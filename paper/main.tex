\title{The conditions for a qudit advantage in%
\texorpdfstring{\\}{ }uncorrected quantum algorithms under decoherence}

\author{John Doxaras}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
Whether qudits buy resilience against decoherence in full algorithms
is open: prior noisy comparisons stop at gate-level criteria, single
arithmetic primitives, or a fixed dimension, and error-correcting
codes have no problem instance to compress. We simulate Shor order
finding, eigenstate phase estimation, and Grover search as bare,
uncorrected circuits on qubit, qutrit, and ququint registers, under a
transmon-calibrated ladder channel and a trapped-ion depolarizing
channel, three entangling-gate cost models, and registers to
Hilbert-space dimension $5.3\times10^{5}$. Two conditions organize
all three algorithms. A qudit advantage needs an entangling gate
whose cost grows at most linearly in $d$: two-level-decomposed gates
forfeit it at $d\ge5$ on the ladder and at $d=7$ everywhere, and the
decomposed qutrit's residual advantage survives realistic accounting
only under per-particle noise. And it survives only while the gate's
own infidelity growth with $d$ stays below a critical inflation
factor we compute: converted through the channel's own damage
identity, the only native gate characterized across $d=2/3/5$
($99.6/98.7/93.7\%$) fails at $d=5$ in seven of eight readings,
while its qutrit gate passes under both charging scopes; the
transmon qutrit gate passes only when charged solely on the
entangler, and loses once its $580$-ns duration is charged as
exposure. Further tightenings independently leave only $d=3$.
Accumulated channel damage fixes end-state fidelity on one
exponential across algorithms and bases; what the algorithm adds is
its decoder, for which we prove an exact finite-size
mediant-interval acceptance law, base-independent at matched control
dimension---the quantum state carries the whole cross-base
difference. A number-theoretic confound, grid alignment, reverses
naive cross-dimension comparisons. A shallow trapped-ion anchor
circuit fixes the channel's scale ($0.617\pm0.007$); deeper anchors
fail coherently, bounding the validated depth.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Quantum hardware is not binary by nature. Transmons, trapped ions,
nitrogen-vacancy centers, and molecular spins all expose more than two
usable levels, and processors that exploit them as qudits now exist on
several
platforms~\cite{ringbauer2022,blok2021,goss2022,low2023,gardill2020,chiesa2024}.
The theoretical case for higher dimensions is old and, at the level of
structure, strong: fault-tolerant stabilizer constructions close cleanly
in prime dimension~\cite{gottesman1999} and the discrete phase-space
structure is cleanest in odd dimension~\cite{gross2006}; the quantum
arithmetic fractional Fourier transform of Ref.~\cite{floratos2024}
exists for odd prime $p$, where the discrete rotation group
$SO_2[\mathbb{Z}_{p^n}]$ is cyclic (the in-place constant multipliers
and quadratic-phase diagonals of the same construction carry over to
any dimension, including $p=2$, whenever the multiplication constant
is a unit);
magic-state distillation protocols exist in every prime
dimension~\cite{campbell2012}, with thresholds and efficiencies that
keep improving with $d$ (within congruence classes mod 3, for primes up
to 17)~\cite{campbell2014}; and qudit memory thresholds rise under
abstract noise models~\cite{marks2017}. A base-$d$ register also compresses algorithms:
at matched problem size it needs $\log_2 d$ times fewer carriers and a
proportionally shorter schedule.

Whether any of this survives \emph{physical} decoherence---in which
higher levels of a real device decay and dephase faster than lower
ones---is a question the literature has flagged repeatedly and not
answered. Campbell noted that ``in physical systems one may also see
noise rise with $d$. Such features depend subtly on the details of the
underlying physics''~\cite{campbell2014}; Marks \emph{et al.} point to
the ``increased degrees of freedom that can be coupled to the
environment'' and call whether such noise can be kept sufficiently
small ``an interesting question for experimental
implementations''~\cite{marks2017}; Pavlidis and Floratos, citing qubit
evidence that order finding tolerates truncation of small QFT
rotations~\cite{barenco1996,nam2012}, conjecture ``a similar
robustness'' for qudits but leave its investigation
open~\cite{pavlidis2021}; and the recent qudit review of Kiktenko
\emph{et al.} lists ``the investigation of the impact of noise within
the discussed schemes'' as an open problem~\cite{kiktenko2025}.
Comparisons \emph{across} dimensions still treat noise indirectly:
entangling-gate counts as a noise
proxy~\cite{nikolaeva2024,kiktenko2025}, analytic coherent-error
bounds~\cite{bocharov2017}, or the qualitative argument that fewer
carriers means less local noise~\cite{wang2020}. Noisy qutrit circuit
simulations exist~\cite{gokhale2019,gustafson2022}, but always at
$d=3$ against a qubit baseline; the closest cross-dimension noisy
study, a QFT-based adder under local noise across arbitrary
$d$~\cite{agrawal2025}, compares a single arithmetic primitive rather
than a full algorithm with its decoder. No study has compared several
prime dimensions on full algorithms under channels calibrated to
measured per-level coherences, or followed such a comparison to scale. A claim
that qudit phase-estimation error decreases exponentially with
$d$---attributed by the review of Wang \emph{et
al.}~\cite{wang2020} to a conference contribution of Parasa and
Perkowski~\cite{parasa2011}, whose available slides state it for the
algorithm's intrinsic failure probability at fixed
precision---concerns the noiseless algorithm; we are not aware of it
having been tested against a noise model.

This paper answers the question quantitatively at the algorithm level.
We simulate Shor order finding~\cite{shor1997}, eigenstate quantum
phase estimation (QPE)~\cite{kitaev1995}, and Grover
search~\cite{grover1996} on qubit ($d=2$), qutrit ($d=3$), and ququint
($d=5$) registers, under two decoherence channels chosen to bracket real
hardware---an anharmonic-ladder channel calibrated to published
per-level transmon coherence measurements
\cite{peterer2015,goss2022,blok2021,tripathi2025,wang2025}, and a
per-particle depolarizing channel representative of trapped-ion
qudits~\cite{ringbauer2022,low2023}---under three entangling-gate cost
models spanning native gates, the measured trapped-ion
$2(d{-}1)$ M{\o}lmer--S{\o}rensen cost~\cite{ringbauer2022}, and
$O(d^2)$ two-level decomposition~\cite{pavlidis2021}, with registers
to Hilbert-space dimension $5.3\times10^{5}$ (19.0 qubit-equivalents; the
widest qubit register is 17 qubits) reached by quantum
trajectories; a demo-size $d=7$ run (Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}) extends the
cost grid one prime higher.

Our central result is a pair of conditions rather than a verdict:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Qudit gates compiled by two-level decomposition ($O(d^2)$ layer
cost on the entanglers) forfeit the advantage at $d\ge5$ on the
calibrated ladder and at $d=7$ in every channel; at $d=3$ the
decomposed circuit is still shorter than the qubit's and the serial
advantage survives both channels, though on the ladder it is erased
by either measured single-qudit costs or concurrent scheduling,
leaving only the per-particle qutrit, thinly
(Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}). A native entangling gate whose cost grows no
faster than linearly in $d$ delivers the advantage when the operating
dephasing is weak or unstructured---but only while the gate's own
per-application infidelity growth with $d$ stays below a critical
inflation factor $f^\ast$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}). Measured
hardware decides the second condition against the ququint in seven
of eight readings and \emph{for} the trapped-ion qutrit in seven of
eight; the transmon qutrit's verdict turns on whether a platform's
idle decoherence scales with its entangling-gate error, and on
whether its entangler is charged in layers or in wall-clock time.
Free-evolution
ladder dephasing further splits the linear-cost cells by algorithm and
dimension, and Zeeman-structured dephasing---higher levels
dephasing tens of times faster---reverses the verdict at every cost
model in its Markovian idealization, though not under laboratory
quasi-static field noise (Secs.~\ref{sec:cost}
and~\ref{sec:robustness}).}
\end{quote}

The condition is a statement about bare, uncorrected circuits---the
regime of every near-term demonstration. It does not transfer
automatically to the error-correction layer, where a code has no
problem instance to compress and the dimension dependence can carry
the opposite sign~\cite{keppens2025} (Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}).

The native-gate cost requirement is met by current trapped-ion qudit
gates---the single-application light-shift entangler of Hrmo \emph{et
al.}~\cite{hrmo2023} realizes it up to $d=5$---and by native
cross-Kerr transmon entanglers~\cite{goss2022}, and violated whenever
qudit gates are compiled by two-level
decomposition~\cite{pavlidis2021,gustafson2025synthesis}. The
infidelity condition is what both measured gates currently fail at
their $d=5$ frontiers: the ion gate's error grows $16\times$
from $d=2$ to $d=5$
($0.004\to0.063$), exceeding
the critical inflation factor at $d=5$ in seven of eight readings,
while its $d=3$ gate now clears the threshold under both charging
scopes; the transmon cross-Kerr gate's measured $97.3\%$ clears
the $d=3$ threshold only when the factor is charged solely on the
entangler---$0.9$ points short of the global-scope target, and
lost outright once its $580$-ns duration is charged against a
two-qubit comparator faster than ${\sim}250$~ns
(Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}).
Together the two conditions explain
the split verdicts in prior work: qutrit advantages obtained with
native third levels---as ancillas~\cite{gokhale2019} or as encoding
capacity~\cite{gustafson2022}---versus 35--69\%
more non-Clifford gates for single-qutrit synthesis from a fault-tolerant
gate set~\cite{gustafson2025synthesis}. An independent analytic gate-level
criterion~\cite{jankovic2024}, which we reproduce numerically to
$4\times10^{-4}$ and apply to our circuits under the identification
of layer-count ratios with gate-efficiency ratios (an assumption
scoped in Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}), is consistent with our
algorithm-level outcomes in four of six cost-model/dimension cases
(all misses in the conservative direction), with break-even
gate-efficiency ratios of $1.68$ ($d=3$), $3.45$ ($d=5$), and $5.70$
($d=7$)---a constant factor of ${\sim}3$ below the naive
$d^2/\log_2 d$ estimate.

Three further results are methodological and, we believe, of independent
use to the field.

\emph{(i) Grid alignment is a confound in every cross-dimension
comparison of order finding} (Sec.~\ref{sec:alignment}). Phase
estimation concentrates probability on the phases $s/r$; when the order
$r$ divides the control dimension $D=d^m$ these sit exactly on
measurement grid points and the peaks are maximally noise-robust. Which
base receives this gift is decided by number theory, not physics. The
canonical demonstration instance $N=15$ admits \emph{only} power-of-two
orders, silently handing qubits perfect alignment; on unbiased instances
the ordering of bases reverses. Alignment predicts the winner in five of the six
biased runs we construct (three aligned instances $\times$ two noise
models); the exception is $N=15$ under depolarizing, where the aligned
qubit trails the ququint by $1.4\sigma$ ($0.72\pm0.05$ against
$0.81\pm0.03$) while holding its lead in the ladder channel---a miss
inside the noise of a 400-trajectory point, but a miss, and we count
it as one. A within-modulus control---two orders
hosted on identical registers---prices it at ${\approx}0.2$ in
floor-corrected signal (a figure measured on prime-order alignment,
where a corollary of the decoder lemma---noiseless aligned success is
exactly $\varphi(r)/r$---shows the gift is largest;
Sec.~\ref{sec:alignment}).

\emph{(ii) The mechanism is width-and-depth compression, and what
survives it is the decoder}
(Secs.~\ref{sec:grover}--\ref{sec:mechanism}). Grover search holds the
oracle count fixed across bases, delivering roughly half of Shor's
exposure compression: the qudit advantage survives (confirming a
prediction recorded in the source before the runs) at $0.33$--$0.50$
of Shor's. Counted in \emph{damage
units}---the per-carrier-layer entanglement infidelity $1-\mathrm{tr}\,
S/d^2$ of the actual channel---accumulated exposure places end-state
fidelity for both algorithm families on a single exponential
($R^2=0.97$--$0.99$ in linear fidelity, $0.76$ rescored in log
fidelity, amplitude ${\approx}1$)---what first-order
composition of incoherent channels requires, rather than a discovery.
What exposure does \emph{not}
determine is the measured success probability, because the classical
decoder intervenes: continued-fraction order recovery gains error
tolerance with register size, and we observe it decoding correct orders
from states with fidelity ${\sim}3\times10^{-4}$ to the ideal state. That
tolerance has a closed form we state and verify---an exact finite-$D$
mediant-interval formula for the acceptance set of a decoder required
to return $r$ itself, built on a proved acceptance lemma and including
the deeper convergents $kr\le N$ that window bounds do not
count---reproducing the enumerated acceptance set outcome-for-outcome
on 27 instance/size combinations, with the lemma checked on 42; its
asymptotic density is a totient sum over the admissible denominators
(Sec.~\ref{sec:decoder}, Appendix~\ref{app:decoder}). Because it turns
on $r$ and $N$ and not on the base, at matched control dimension the
whole cross-base difference in decoded success sits in the quantum
state.

\emph{(iii) Standing reviewer objections are pre-empted with data}
(Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}): $d$-dependent readout error is structurally
near-neutral (the growth of per-level misread rates with $d$ cancels
against the reduced carrier count), and refocused operation helps qudits
\emph{more} than qubits in all conditions tested, moving the break-even
point of the central condition by roughly one cost model. The same
section stress-tests the noise conventions themselves: against
inflated per-layer qudit noise, against Zeeman-structured dephasing
(where the condition genuinely fails unmitigated), and against
composite dimensions $d=4$ and $6$.

Finally, the qubit branch of the predictions is checked on real
hardware (Sec.~\ref{sec:hardware}): on a commercial trapped-ion
processor the shallow compiled circuit lands inside its predicted
band---a single-point consistency check of the depolarizing
convention on the platform it models---while the deep-circuit and
fixed-connectivity failures delineate, on hardware, the
coherent-systematic and compilation-overhead regimes our channels
deliberately exclude, bounding the convention's validated depth range.

Prior work closest to ours is differentiated in
Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}: the gate-level criterion of Jankovi\'c
\emph{et al.}~\cite{jankovic2024} (no algorithms, no scaling), the qutrit
circuit studies of Gokhale \emph{et al.}~\cite{gokhale2019} and
Gustafson~\cite{gustafson2022} ($d=3$ only; no comparison across
multiple prime dimensions, no per-level-calibrated channel), the
noisy cross-dimension adder of Agrawal \emph{et
al.}~\cite{agrawal2025} (one primitive, no decoder, no calibrated
channel), and the
code-level comparison of Keppens \emph{et al.}~\cite{keppens2025}, whose
opposite ordering we reconcile explicitly: a code has no problem instance
to compress, so it pays the per-carrier cost of dimension without the
width-and-depth rebate that is the entire source of the algorithmic
advantage measured here.

\subsection{Algorithms and registers}

We compare three algorithms chosen to span the failure modes of
interference under decoherence. \emph{Shor order finding}: given $N$ and
a coprime $a$, find the multiplicative order $r$ of $a \bmod N$, using a
control register of $m$ base-$d$ digits (control dimension $D=d^m$) and a
work register of $w=\lceil\log_d N\rceil$ digits; success is the
probability that continued-fraction post-processing of the measured
outcome recovers $r$ exactly. Our benchmark instances are unbiased in the
sense of Sec.~\ref{sec:alignment}: $N=21$, $a=2$ ($r=6$) and $N=29$,
$a=16$ ($r=7$). \emph{Eigenstate QPE}: phase estimation on an
eigenstate of a diagonal unitary with target phase equal to the
golden-ratio conjugate $\varphi^\ast\approx0.6180$, chosen to be far
from every fraction with a small base-2, -3, or -5 denominator; success
is the estimate landing within $2^{-(b+1)}$ of the target ($b=5$ bits).
\emph{Grover search} over $M=d^n$ items with
$\lfloor(\pi/4)\sqrt{M}\rceil$ iterations; success is the probability of
measuring the marked item, averaged over a sample of marked items
excluding $\lvert 0\cdots0\rangle$ (the diffuser's own reflection axis
and the bottom of the damping ladder).

Because $M=d^n$ never matches exactly across bases, all cross-base
comparisons are made at matched problem size by interpolating each
base's results onto a common $\log_2$-size axis (linear interpolation
of the signal in $\log_2$ size); comparing raw
demo-size points reverses orderings and is one of the two fairness traps
we document (the other is alignment).

\subsection{Noise channels}
\label{sec:channels}

One time-layer of noise is applied to \emph{every} carrier for every
layer of the serial schedule; a gate spanning $k$ carriers occupies its
decomposition depth in layers (below), so idling carriers decohere while
gates execute.

\emph{Calibrated ladder (transmon-like).} A Lindblad channel with
sequential relaxation $\Gamma_k \propto k^{0.7}$ and pure dephasing
following a max-level law $\Gamma_\phi(j,k)\propto\max(j,k)^{1.1}$,
exponentiated exactly per layer. Both exponents are fits to published
per-level coherence data spanning nine devices and $d=3$ to $12$: the
relaxation ratio $\Gamma_2/\Gamma_1$ is measured at ${\approx}1.7$
\cite{goss2022,blok2021,tripathi2025,peterer2015,yurtalan2020,wang2025}
against $2.0$ for the
textbook $\Gamma_k\propto k$ ladder, and the dephasing ratios
$\Gamma_\phi^{01}:\Gamma_\phi^{12}:\Gamma_\phi^{02}$ are measured at
$1:2.0:2.3$---incompatible with the textbook $(\Delta\mathrm{level})^2$
law (which predicts $1:1:4$) because the charge dispersion of
$\lvert2\rangle$ exceeds that of $\lvert1\rangle$ by an order of
magnitude~\cite{blok2021}. The max-level dephasing target is realized
\emph{exactly} by diagonal jump operators obtained through classical
multidimensional scaling (Sec.~\ref{sec:methods}); our channel
reproduces the measured ratios as $1.62$ and $1:2.14:2.14$. Rates are
normalized so the $0{\leftrightarrow}1$ subspace is bit-for-bit
identical to the qubit channel: every difference between bases is
purely a higher-level effect. A dephasing-scale knob interpolates to the
high-$E_J/E_C$ regime demonstrated at
$d=12$~\cite{wang2025}, where echo coherence approaches the $T_1$
limit; the same knob models refocused operation
(Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}).

\emph{Per-particle depolarizing (ion-like).} $\rho \to (1-p)\rho + p\,
\mathbb{I}/d$ per carrier-layer. The convention rests on measured
trapped-ion structure: every qudit level is ground-state or metastable
($\tau_1\sim1.1$\,s, orders of magnitude beyond gate times),
allowed-transition magnetic sensitivities span only a factor of
${\sim}5$ (pair dephasing \emph{rates} go as the square, and idling
pairs beyond the driven set widen the span---Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}
prices the full anisotropy, a $1$--$49\times$ rate spread at $d=5$),
and the single-qudit \emph{per-pulse} error is nearly flat in $d$
($2.0\times10^{-4}$ at $d=3$, $3.2\times10^{-4}$ at $d=5$; the
per-\emph{Clifford} error rises $5\times$, $2\times10^{-3}$ to
$1.0\times10^{-2}$, because a $d=5$ Clifford takes ${\sim}3\times$
the pulses)~\cite{ringbauer2022}, with qudit control demonstrated to
13 levels~\cite{low2023}. A Zeeman-structured dephasing variant that
keeps the encoding's full pair anisotropy is tested in
Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}.

Measured per-gate infidelities place transmon two-qutrit gates at
$2.7\times10^{-2}$--$4.8\times10^{-2}$ (CZ$^\dagger$/CZ process
infidelity~\cite{goss2022}) and ion two-qudit
entangling gates at $1.3\times10^{-2}$--$6.3\times10^{-2}$ for
$d=3$--$5$ (native light-shift~\cite{hrmo2023}) and
$2.5\times10^{-2}$--$6.2\times10^{-2}$ at $d=3$
(decomposed~\cite{ringbauer2022}); only single-qudit pulses reach the
$10^{-3}$--$10^{-4}$ scale~\cite{ringbauer2022,low2023}. In the
per-carrier-layer convention above, a measured gate infidelity
$\epsilon$ over $L$ layers implies $s=\epsilon/[2L(1-1/d^2)]$; our
sweeps ($0.001$--$0.05$ at demo size, $0.003$--$0.005$ for scaling)
bracket the implied operating points, which are marked on every
figure, and Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness} evaluates the grid at the
per-base strengths the measured gates actually imply.

Four named noise regimes appear in the figures: the \emph{idealized
ladder} ($\Gamma_k\propto k$, $(\Delta\mathrm{level})^2$ dephasing---the
textbook model, used only as a bound), the \emph{calibrated ladder}
above, its \emph{low-charge-dispersion} variant (the dephasing knob set
to the high-$E_J/E_C$ regime of Ref.~\cite{wang2025}), and
\emph{per-particle depolarizing}.

\subsection{Gate-cost models}

Real hardware charges more per entangling gate as $d$ grows, and this is
the strongest objection to any qudit advantage. We charge three
published cost structures as layer multipliers: \texttt{uniform} (native
qudit entangler, one layer regardless of $d$---e.g.\ the cross-Kerr CZ
of Ref.~\cite{goss2022} or the single-application light-shift gate of
Ref.~\cite{hrmo2023}); \texttt{ion} ($d-1$ per entangling gate: the
supplement of Ref.~\cite{ringbauer2022} decomposes a $d$-dimensional
controlled-increment into $2(d{-}1)$ pairwise fully entangling gates,
which we normalize to $1$ at $d=2$, the $d{=}2$ MS gate being itself
the two-pulse unit---the same reference's main text quotes $2d$ MS
gates for the Cinc and a $d$-\emph{independent} two-gate cost for the
controlled-exchange, so linear-in-$d$ is the representative middle of
the measured constructions rather than a unique count); and
\texttt{pavlidis} ($d^2/4$ on
two-qudit gates, the harshest of the three: the \emph{controlled}
rotations of the
QFT-arithmetic circuits of Ref.~\cite{pavlidis2021} decompose into
$4(d-1)^2$ elementary two-level gates, i.e.\ $(d-1)^2$ per gate after
the same $d=2$ normalization, and our $d^2/4$ charge rounds that cost
\emph{down}---$2.25$ against $4$ at $d=3$, $6.25$ against $16$ at
$d=5$; the same reference's Table~1 carries the $d^2$ factor in
circuit \emph{depth} as well ($8d^2q$ for the QFT, $4d^2q$ per
adder), and the follow-up construction of Ref.~\cite{floratos2024}
retains it ($16p^2n$ for the in-place constant multiplier). The
$d^2$ is the two-level decomposition cost of a controlled rotation;
a \emph{single}-qudit rotation decomposes into $O(d)$ two-level
gates, not $O(d^2)$, so it stays at one layer here and its measured
cost is priced separately in Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}). The three
models swing the ququint Shor circuit from $3.8\times$ \emph{shorter}
than the qubit's (15 vs 57 layers on $N=21$) to $1.1\times$
\emph{longer} (62.2 vs 57)---and that swing, not the noise model,
does most to decide the winner. These counts are \emph{layers}, not
wall-clock time; Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness} charges measured gate
durations and reports where that changes the verdict. Multi-qudit gates in Grover (oracle and reflection)
are applied as exact unitaries but charged their $n-1$-layer
decomposition depth; charging them one layer would hand the largest free
ride to the base packing the most carriers into the gate, which is
$d=2$. All three models charge a \emph{single}-qudit gate one layer at
every $d$, which matches the per-pulse benchmarking of
Ref.~\cite{ringbauer2022} but not its per-Clifford figures; since the
single-qudit share of the schedule grows with $d$ ($21\%/31\%/40\%$ at
$d=2/3/5$), that omission favors qudits, and
Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness} charges it and reports the threshold at
which each cell fails. Unless a figure states otherwise, plotted results use the
\texttt{uniform} (native-entangler) cost model---the most
qudit-favorable of the three; Table~\ref{tab:cost} and
Secs.~\ref{sec:cost}, \ref{sec:grover}, and~\ref{sec:robustness}
quantify how the winner changes under \texttt{ion} and
\texttt{pavlidis}.

\subsection{Success metric}

Raw success probabilities have base- and instance-dependent random
floors: a uniformly random outcome passes continued fractions with
probability $0.12$--$0.13$ on the $N=21$ benchmark at demo size,
$0.20$--$0.26$ on $N=29$, and up to $0.59$ on the scorable
small-order instances (at $r=2$ the floor reaches $0.72$--$0.80$,
meeting or exceeding the noiseless baseline).
All comparisons therefore use the floor-corrected signal
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{signal} \;=\;
\frac{\mathrm{success}-\mathrm{floor}}
     {\mathrm{success}_{\mathrm{noiseless}}-\mathrm{floor}},
\end{equation}
which is $1$ for perfect interference, $0$ for random guessing, and
negative when noise actively biases outcomes away from the answer.
Floors and noiseless baselines are recomputed for every base, size, and
readout-error setting. Because small orders compress the metric's
dynamic range, we require a floor-to-baseline span above $0.15$ for
any instance used in a quantitative comparison; the one exception is
the $r=3$ aligned instance of Fig.~\ref{fig:alignment} (spans
$0.07$--$0.12$), retained because no other instance represents its
alignment class---its winner margins far exceed its metric
compression, but we quote no signal magnitudes from it.

\section{Grid alignment: a confound that decides winners}
\label{sec:alignment}

Phase estimation concentrates probability on the phases $s/r$. If $r$
divides $D=d^m$, these sit exactly on grid points of the base-$d$
measurement basis and the interference peaks are perfectly sharp;
otherwise they smear over neighboring outcomes, and smeared peaks are
far more fragile under noise. Which base receives sharp peaks is decided
by the arithmetic of the instance, not the physics of the hardware---and
it is invisible in the existing literature because prior studies work at
fixed $d$, where alignment is a constant.

The canonical instance $N=15$ is maximally pathological: its
multiplicative group is $\mathbb{Z}_2\times\mathbb{Z}_4$, so
\emph{every} order is a power of two and the qubit register always lands
exactly on grid while $d=3,5$ never do. Any cross-dimension comparison
run on $N=15$---including the first version of this study---hands base 2
a structural gift unrelated to decoherence.

Figure~\ref{fig:alignment} shows one instance per alignment class. The
aligned base wins in \textbf{five of six} biased runs, with margins
exceeding anything decoherence produces between bases (at $r=5$ the
aligned ququint scores $1.00$ against the qubit's $0.25$--$0.43$); the
exception is $N=15$ under depolarizing noise, where the aligned qubit
trails the ququint by $1.4\sigma$ ($0.72\pm0.05$ against
$0.81\pm0.03$) while holding its lead in the ladder channel. On
the two unbiased instances ($r=6$, $r=7$) all four runs put both
qudits above the qubit at ${\ge}5\sigma$; the $d=5>d=3$ leg is
decisive under depolarizing ($3.9\sigma$ and $4.1\sigma$) but a
statistical tie on the ladder ($1.1\sigma$ at $N=21$, $0.3\sigma$ at
$N=29$). Misalignment is moreover graded, not binary:
averaging the distance of the phases $s/r$ to the nearest grid point,
the $r=6$ instance is mildly tilted \emph{toward} the qubit ($0.267$ vs
$0.300$) and the qubit still loses by $0.21$--$0.48$, while $r=7$
($N=29$) is exactly neutral across all three bases---the cleanest
instance we know of, and the one we recommend for future benchmarks.

\emph{Within-modulus control.} Varying $N$ also varies register widths,
so we isolate alignment with two moduli that each host an aligned and an
unaligned order on \emph{identical} registers: $N=33$ and $N=55$ carry
both $r=5$ (exact in base 5) and $r=10$ (exact in no base) with the same
$(m,w)$, gate counts, and noise exposure per base. Removing alignment
costs the ququint $0.15$--$0.22$ signal and narrows its lead over the
qubit by $0.09$--$0.21$, consistently across both moduli and both noise
models---while the ququint's \emph{residual} lead on the unaligned
orders remains $0.37$--$0.59$, roughly twice the alignment term. Both effects
are real; alignment is worth on the order of $0.15$--$0.2$ signal to whoever receives
it, and the remaining qudit lead is physical.

\emph{The aligned ceiling $\varphi(r)/r$.} Alignment interacts with
the decoder, and the interaction has a closed form that follows in one
line from the acceptance lemma of Appendix~\ref{app:decoder}. When
$r\mid D$, the ideal distribution is supported on the $r$ outcomes
$y=sD/r$; the phase $s/r$ is an exact rational whose convergent
denominators terminate at $r/\gcd(s,r)$, so by the lemma the decoder
accepts exactly the $s$ coprime to $r$:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Corollary. For a perfectly aligned instance the noiseless
decoded success probability is exactly $\varphi(r)/r$.}
\end{quote}

This converts the scorability pattern of Table~\ref{tab:ensemble}
from an empirical observation into a theorem. Base-2 alignment forces
$r=2^k$, and $\varphi(2^k)/2^k=1/2$ \emph{identically}---every
base-2-aligned instance has a noiseless ceiling of one half, at any
modulus and any register size, which is why its floor meets its
baseline and no converse base-2 control can ever be constructed
($N=15$ is the only qubit-aligned instance with usable dynamic range
for a different reason: its floors happen to sit low). The corollary
also scopes the ${\approx}0.2$ alignment price: all three aligned
instances tested have \emph{prime} $r$, where
$\varphi(r)/r=1-1/r$ is near-maximal and the decoder rejects almost
nothing; for $r=p^k$ the gift shrinks to $1-1/p$, and the alignment
price quoted above is therefore calibrated on the alignment class
where it is largest. The quantity that decides the gift is grid
alignment \emph{weighted by} $\varphi(r)/r$, not alignment alone.

\emph{Ensemble over the multiplicative group.} Shor draws $a$

Table~\ref{tab:cost} is the paper's central result in one table. Its
cells are exact density matrices, so they carry no statistical
uncertainty; the axis that matters is the strength they are evaluated
at. Sweeping it across the demo grid
($0.001$--$0.05$; \texttt{results/cost\_grid\_ssweep.json}) leaves the
qudit-versus-qubit verdict of \emph{every} cell unchanged wherever the
metric still has dynamic range---we judge only strengths at which the
best base clears $0.10$ signal, beyond which all three bases sit at the
random floor and the ranking is decided by third-decimal noise. That
cutoff leaves live windows narrower than the grid: $0.001$--$0.02$
for the \texttt{uniform} cells, $0.001$--$0.01$ for
depolarizing/\texttt{ion} and \texttt{pavlidis}, and only
$0.001$--$0.005$ for their ladder counterparts, so the stability
claim is scoped to those windows. One
piece of over-precision in the table should be read down, however: in
the depolarizing/\texttt{ion} cell the two qudits are tied rather than
ordered ($0.502$ against $0.497$, and the ordering reverses to $d=3$ at
$s\le0.002$), so that cell establishes that a qudit wins, not which.
The
decomposition direction of the condition holds in a weakened,
dimension-graded form. Under $d^2$-per-entangler decomposition
(\texttt{pavlidis}; single-qudit gates cost one layer,
Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}) the serial demo grid no longer hands the
qubit a sweep: the qutrit beats the qubit on \emph{both} channels
($0.461$ vs $0.331$ per-particle, $0.333$ vs $0.282$ on the ladder;
replicated at 1000-trajectory statistics in the $d=7$ demo grid,
$0.445$ vs $0.338$ and $0.339$ vs $0.290$), the ququint splits
($0.361$ wins per-particle, $0.132$ loses the ladder), and $d=7$
loses everywhere. The grading tracks the layer table. At $d=3$ the
corrected surcharge still leaves the circuit \emph{shorter} than the
qubit's ($48.5$ vs $57$ layers), so decomposition trims the qutrit's
margin without surrendering either compression; at $d=5$ the
surcharge overtakes the depth compression ($62.2$ vs $57$) and only
the width compression (5 vs 11 carriers) survives---enough under
nearly-flat per-particle damage, not under the ladder's escalating
per-event damage; by $d=7$ neither survives. Eigenstate QPE moves
with Shor (its per-particle \texttt{pavlidis} cell is now a qudit
win, $0.469$ against $0.396$, no longer a dead heat, while its
ladder cell stays with the qubit), and Grover, whose compression is
halved, is where decomposition still bites hardest
(Sec.~\ref{sec:grover}). Two caveats keep the serial
\texttt{pavlidis} qutrit wins from reading as a working
no-native-gate route. On the ladder the win is an artifact of the
uncharged serial schedule: it dies at single-qudit charge
$\alpha^\ast=1.44$, below any measured value, and flips to the qubit
under concurrent scheduling (Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}). Under
per-particle noise it is real but thin: it survives concurrency
($+0.130\to+0.076$) and holds up to the top of the measured
single-qudit-cost range ($\alpha^\ast=3.17$ against measured
$2.15$--$3.15$). Under a native qudit gate
(\texttt{uniform}) the width-and-depth compression is untouched and
qudits win on both hardware classes. The intermediate linear cost
(\texttt{ion}) is where dephasing level decides: on per-particle
hardware the qudit advantage survives (the tied qutrit/ququint pair at
$0.497$/$0.502$ against $0.331$), while at
free-evolution ladder dephasing the verdict splits---the qutrit still
wins Shor but the ququint loses ($0.256$ vs $0.282$), eigenstate QPE is
a dead heat (advantage $+0.00$), and Grover passes to the qubit for
both qudits (Sec.~\ref{sec:grover}). Refocusing repairs this entire
column (Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}). Eigenstate QPE (never confounded,
its golden-ratio target phase being irrational in every base) otherwise
tracks Shor, with advantages of $+0.42$ (\texttt{uniform}) and $+0.20$
(\texttt{ion}) on per-particle noise. The decomposition direction is
therefore no longer a clean forfeit: it fails outright at $d\ge5$ on
the ladder and at $d=7$ everywhere, fails at $d=3$ once measured
single-qudit costs or concurrency are charged on the ladder, and is
survived---thinly---by the decomposed qutrit under per-particle
noise.

Cost model and noise model are not independent: each pairing describes a
platform, and both \emph{physically matched} pairings favor
ququints---trapped-ion qudits (\texttt{ion} cost, per-particle noise)
and native-cross-Kerr transmons (\texttt{uniform} cost, calibrated
ladder). The ion pairing presumes shielded, refocused operation;
Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness} prices the unmitigated alternative, and it
is severe. The pessimistic cells of the grid are mostly mismatched
combinations; the genuine failure mode remains the absence of a native
entangler (\texttt{pavlidis})---fatal at $d\ge5$ on the ladder and
under any realistic accounting at $d=3$ there, survivable only by the
per-particle qutrit---which is precisely the regime of
synthesis-based compilation~\cite{gustafson2025synthesis,venturelli2025}.

\emph{Matched control dimension.} The demo grid compares
$D=64/81/125$, and Sec.~\ref{sec:decoder} shows decoder tolerance
grows with $D$---the ququint enters Table~\ref{tab:cost} with twice
the qubit's acceptance set ($16$ vs $8$ outcomes). Equalizing $D$
resolves the worry in the qudits' favor: at $d=2$, $m=7$ ($D=128$,
matching the ququint's $125$) the qubit scores $0.47/0.30$
(ladder/depolarizing) against $0.51/0.33$ at $D=64$---its extra
decoder tolerance is outweighed by the deeper circuit's added
exposure---while the ququint holds $0.76/0.78$ and the qutrit is
stable between $D=81$ and $243$ ($0.73/0.67$ vs $0.71/0.66$).
Unequal $D$ biases the demo grid against the qudits, not for them.

An independent check: Jankovi\'c \emph{et al.}~\cite{jankovic2024}
derive, by linear response over Haar-random gates under pure dephasing,
the critical gate-efficiency ratio $(d^2-1)/(3\log_2 d)$ a single
qudit must clear to beat a multi-qubit register at matched Hilbert
dimension---$1.68$ at $d=3$, $3.45$ at $d=5$, and $5.70$ at $d=7$.
Their criterion is stated for ratios of gate \emph{times} in units of
the decoherence time; reading our layer-count ratios as
gate-efficiency ratios identifies the two, which holds when per-layer
duration and coherence time scale in the same ratio across
bases---an assumption, not a derived fact, and one the structured
channels of Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness} can violate. Under that
identification the gate-level
criterion is consistent with our algorithm-level winner in four of six
cost/dimension cases on the ladder channel
(\texttt{results/jankovic.json}), including the tight case
($d=5$ \texttt{uniform} clears $3.45$ at $3.80$ and wins). Both misses
($d=3$ \texttt{ion} at ratio $1.30$, and $d=3$ \texttt{pavlidis} at
$1.18$, each below the $1.68$ bar yet winning) are in the
conservative direction their pure-dephasing assumption predicts, our
calibrated relaxation being gentler ($\Gamma_k\propto k^{0.7}$); the
$d=7$ \texttt{uniform} case below misses the same way.

\emph{The seventh dimension.} A demo-size $d=7$ run on the unbiased
instance (1000 trajectories per point, both channels, all three cost
models; base 7 has the same mean residual misalignment, $0.300$, as
bases 3 and 5) extends the grid one prime higher and sharpens the
condition. A caveat on the cost inputs: measured qudit entangling
gates stop at $d=5$~\cite{hrmo2023} (Ringbauer's are characterized
at $d=3$~\cite{ringbauer2022}), so at $d=7$ every cost model is an
extrapolation beyond any measurement. The decomposition verdict is
unambiguous at this dimension: \texttt{pavlidis} loses to the qubit in
all three conditions ($-0.05$ to $+0.17$ signal against the qubit's
$0.29$--$0.49$), the seventh prime being where decomposition finally
forfeits the advantage outright even in the serial schedule. The
native-gate direction survives---under \texttt{uniform} cost the $d=7$
register beats the qubit on both channels ($0.52$ vs $0.24$ on the
calibrated ladder, $0.77$ vs $0.33$ under depolarizing, at
$s=0.005$)---but on the ladder the ordering is no longer monotone in
$d$: the optimum sits at $d=5$ ($0.64$), the larger per-event damage
of the seventh level pulling $d=7$ back to $0.52$, while under
depolarizing $d=7$ ($0.772\pm0.018$) is statistically tied with
$d=5$ ($0.783\pm0.015$; a $0.5\sigma$ margin) and above the rest. The intermediate
\texttt{ion} cost, already split at $d=5$, now fails cleanly on the
ladder ($0.05$ vs $0.25$) and retains only a $+0.05$ ($1.6\sigma$)
edge under depolarizing: the window between native and decomposed cost narrows
with every added level, exactly as the rising break-even curve
suggests. The criterion itself is conservative here---its $d=7$ bar of
$5.70$ is not cleared (the uniform layer ratio is $3.80$), yet the
$d=7$ register wins.

because the qubit decays several times faster per precision bit---a

dependence is the shallowest in every regime, but it is flat in none of

therefore a summary rather than a model. Under the calibrated ladder the
first three sizes agree within $0.013$ ($0.705$--$0.718$
from 6.3 to 9.5 bits) and the whole
decline is carried by the last
three, the 14.3-bit point sitting $3.5\sigma$ below the 9.5-bit one.
Because both statements are spent on the same error bars, we
characterized the trajectory estimator rather than assuming it
(Sec.~\ref{sec:methods}): across $24$ independent $1000$-trajectory
replicas at five grid points the empirical standard error of the mean
agrees with the quoted one to a ratio of $0.995$ (range
$0.84$--$1.17$; no point departs at $p<0.2$), so the bars are
calibrated, not conservative. A constant fit to the plateau gives
$\chi^2/\mathrm{dof}=0.15$ ($p=0.14$), which is unremarkable; we
quote the raw spread there and reserve significance for the fall.
An earlier release of these sweeps returned
$\chi^2/\mathrm{dof}=0.015$ on the same three points, a $1.5\%$
low-tail value that might be read as over-conservative bars. It was
not: regenerating every sweep from reproducible seeds
(Sec.~\ref{sec:methods}) returns the ordinary value above, which is
what a fluctuation does and what a mis-sized error model would not.
The same shape appears under depolarizing, where four sizes hold near
$0.65$ before falling to $0.59\pm0.04$ at 14.3 bits, a $2.0\sigma$ drop
from 9.5 bits that turns a slope consistent with zero over the first
five sizes into $-0.007\pm0.004$/bit over six. The onset is later and
the fall gentler in the channel whose per-event damage is flatter in
$d$, which is the ordering Sec.~\ref{sec:mechanism} predicts: the
decoder tolerance of Sec.~\ref{sec:decoder} postpones the decay of
decoded success but does not outrun it. We report the plateau as an
observation with a mechanism, not a law: five sizes would have read as
a flat depolarizing family, and it is the sixth that resolves the
shape. One objection must be met first: $D=d^m$ changes across the
sweep, so the plateau could be residual grid misalignment drifting
with $m$---the confound of Sec.~\ref{sec:alignment} returning in a new
form. It is not. Residual misalignment is \emph{exactly constant}
across the entire sweep for all three bases, on both benchmark
instances ($0.267$ for $d=2$ and $0.300$ for $d=3,5$ at $N=21$;
$0.286$ for every base at $N=29$; constant to numerical precision,
spread ${<}10^{-11}$, over the
sizes), because $d^m \bmod r$ is periodic in $m$ and the multiset of
phase offsets it induces therefore repeats. Alignment does not vary
with register size here, so it can explain neither a size-dependent
nor a size-independent signal. On the fitted curves the qutrit
overtakes the ququint at 8.6 bits ($68\%$ CI $8.1$--$9.1$,
calibrated ladder) and 12.5 bits ($68\%$ CI $11.3$--$14.7$,
depolarizing---an extrapolation, the ququint's largest simulated
size being 11.6 bits, though the interval's lower end falls just
inside it),
and from the
smallest common size under the idealized ladder---the story is not
monotonic in $d$, and the best base depends on the target precision.
Second, for eigenstate QPE the qudit advantage is decisive and grows
with size: at 11.6 bits the ququint retains $2.7\times$ the qubit's
signal as measured ($+0.42\pm0.02$) and the margin reaches
$+0.46\pm0.02$ ($2.1\times$) in the high-$E_J/E_C$ regime. Quoted
errors are statistical; the dominant uncertainty is systematic---the
spread across channel and cost-model choices documented above. Since
eigenstate QPE is the quantum-chemistry workhorse, this is the result
with the clearest practical consequence.

\emph{Instance robustness.} Re-running the identical Shor sweep on the
second unbiased instance ($N=29$, $a=16$, $r=7$---the exactly
alignment-neutral benchmark of Sec.~\ref{sec:alignment}, whose work
register is one digit wider for $d=3,5$; 400 trajectories per point)
replicates every claim above. Both qudits again clear the
qubit at every size in all four regimes, and the qubit again decays
fastest ($-0.042\pm0.006$/bit under the calibrated ladder, matching
$-0.049\pm0.005$ at $N=21$ within $1.0\sigma$; the ququint matches at
$-0.058\pm0.009$ vs $-0.043\pm0.006$, $1.4\sigma$). Slopes are
compared over \emph{matched} size ranges: the $N=29$ sweep reaches
four qutrit sizes and the $N=21$ sweep six, and setting a four-size
slope against a six-size one that includes the fall is not a
replication test. Restricted to the common range, the qutrit's slope
agrees between the instances under the calibrated ladder
($-0.028\pm0.008$/bit on $N=29$ against $-0.012\pm0.006$ at $N=21$,
$1.7\sigma$) but \emph{not} under depolarizing
($-0.039\pm0.008$ against $-0.001\pm0.007$, $3.7\sigma$), where the
$N=29$ qutrit decays while the $N=21$ qutrit is flat across the same
four sizes. The qutrit remains the shallowest family in every regime
on both instances, which is the ordering claim; its \emph{rate} is
instance-dependent in the flatter channel, and we do not claim
otherwise. The $N=29$ sweep also stops at four qutrit sizes, so it
does not reach the register depth at which the plateau above gives
way. What is instance-robust is the ordering, the
qubit's fastest decay,
and the qutrit-over-ququint crossing under the calibrated ladder,
which recurs at 10.5 bits on $N=29$ (8.6 at $N=21$). Under
depolarizing the crossing is not instance-robust: on $N=29$ the
qutrit decays \emph{faster} than the ququint---the slope
non-replication above---so no crossing occurs at any size.

\section{Grover search: decomposing the mechanism}
\label{sec:grover}

Everything above measures one algorithm family---the interpolation
experiment of Sec.~\ref{sec:mechanism} shows Shor \emph{is} phase
estimation on an eigenstate superposition. Grover search is the second
family, chosen to attack our own weakest claim, with the prediction
registered in advance: \emph{qudits win, by less than in phase
estimation}. The iteration count $\lfloor(\pi/4)\sqrt{M}\rceil$ is
base-independent, so Grover holds the oracle count fixed across bases;
its residual depth compression comes only from the narrower
multi-qudit decompositions, and at matched size its total exposure
compression is roughly half of Shor's ($5.7\times$ vs $10.9\times$
from $d=2$ to $d=5$). This is a partial rather than a clean
separation of width from depth, but it halves the compression while
holding algorithm structure fixed. A null result would have meant the
advantage was depth all along.

\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{grover.png}
\caption{Grover vs Shor floor-corrected signal at matched problem size,

\label{fig:grover}
\end{figure*}

The prediction holds (Fig.~\ref{fig:grover}): the ordering
$d=5>d=3>d=2$ at every matched size in both noise models, with the
Grover advantage at $0.33$--$0.50$ of Shor's. Halving the exposure
compression thus costs one-half to two-thirds of the advantage: the
compressed register is sufficient for a qudit advantage, and the
advantage responds at least proportionally to compression. Grover also
serves as an independent methodological control: it has no order, no
continued fractions, and an exact $1/M$ floor, so grid alignment is
structurally impossible---its agreement rules out the
continued-fraction metric as the source of the effect. The cost
condition binds more tightly here than for Shor: qudits win under
\texttt{uniform}, while under \texttt{pavlidis} the ququint loses at
every matched size in both channels and the qutrit is competitive
only below ${\approx}6$ bits (from ${\approx}6$ bits the ladder is
the qubit's at matched size, and the per-particle qutrit no better
than tied)---Grover keeps none of the decomposed-qutrit advantage
that Shor retains (Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}). Under \texttt{ion} cost at
free-evolution ladder dephasing the
ququint loses Grover at every matched size and the qutrit above
${\approx}6.5$ bits (below which it retains a ${\sim}3\sigma$ edge)
while the qutrit still wins Shor---the cell
where the three algorithms part ways, consistent with Grover's halved
compression buying less headroom against the same gate surcharge, and
the cell that refocusing repairs (Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}).

\section{The mechanism: damage units and the decoder}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\subsection{Entanglement: a falsified hypothesis}

Shor entangles control and work registers; eigenstate QPE does not. An
early hypothesis attributed the (confounded) Shor/QPE difference to
which-path dephasing through the work register. Because Shor's work
input $\lvert x{=}1\rangle$ is an equal superposition of the $r$
eigenstates of the modular multiplier, QPE on a $K$-fold eigenstate
superposition interpolates continuously from eigenstate QPE ($K=1$) to
the Shor regime ($K=r$) with circuit, metric, noise, and cost held
fixed; the control--work entanglement entropy is exactly $\log_2 K$
bits (the sweep reaches $K=4$, i.e.\ $2.0$ of the Shor regime's
$\log_2 6\approx2.6$ bits). The measured effect---the slope of the ququint advantage in
entanglement bits---is far too small, where it exists at all:
consistent with zero under native-gate cost ($+0.002\pm0.012$ per bit
on the calibrated ladder, $-0.011\pm0.011$ under depolarizing) and
$-0.032$ to $-0.043$ ($\pm0.013$) under \texttt{ion} cost, bounding
the effect over the full two-bit range below ${\approx}0.09$
signal against the ${\approx}0.5$ gap---measured on the then-current,
confounded $N=15$ comparison---it was meant to explain (that gap
itself did not survive the de-confounding of
Sec.~\ref{sec:alignment}). We report it as a
falsified mechanism---and it was chasing this failure that exposed the
grid-alignment confound of Sec.~\ref{sec:alignment}.

\subsection{Exposure in damage units, and the decoder boundary}

The surviving mechanism candidate is total noise exposure: carriers
$\times$ time-layers. If it were sufficient, signal would be a function
of exposure $\times$ strength alone and all points---both algorithms,
all bases, all sizes---would collapse onto one curve. They do not
($R^2=0.67$/$0.77$ for ladder/depolarizing), and the failure decomposes
into two identifiable pieces.

\emph{Piece 1: exposure must be counted in damage, not events.} One
noise layer does $d$-dependent harm. Writing $S$ for the one-layer
superoperator, the per-carrier-layer entanglement infidelity is
$1-\Fe$ with $\Fe=\mathrm{tr}\,S/d^2$: under the calibrated ladder at
strength $s$ it is $0.75s$, $1.46s$, and $2.82s$ for $d=2,3,5$---a
ququint takes ${\sim}4\times$ a qubit's damage per event---and
$p(1-1/d^2)$ under depolarizing. With the abscissa
$\mathrm{exposure}\times(1-\Fe)$, Grover's three bases collapse onto a
single exponential: per-family decay rates $0.44/0.49/0.43$ (a $1.1\times$
spread, against $3.6\times$ in event units), each family log-linear with
$R^2\ge0.996$. The reweighting is a ladder effect: depolarizing
damage is already nearly flat in $d$ ($0.75p$--$0.96p$), and there
the signal rows of Table~\ref{tab:collapse} gain nothing from it.

\emph{Piece 2: the residual is the decoder, not the noise.} With units
fixed, a nested fit finds one shared amplitude with a per-\emph{algorithm}
decay rate at $R^2=0.953$ on the ladder and $0.93$--$0.94$ under
depolarizing, the range spanning the event- and damage-unit abscissae
(\texttt{results/exposure\_collapse.json}, \texttt{nested} fits). The
split is stark
at the family level: Grover is a textbook exponential in damage, while
Shor's $d=3$ family holds a flat signal of ${\approx}0.73$ while its
exposure triples. A candidate explanation is that Grover's ``decoder'' is the
identity---its signal \emph{is} marked-state survival---whereas Shor's
signal passes through continued-fraction order recovery, whose error
tolerance grows with register size: added digits add exposure but also
add redundancy the decoder exploits.

This is directly testable: if the decoder story is right, end-state
\emph{fidelity} $\langle\psi_{\mathrm{ideal}}\rvert\rho\lvert
\psi_{\mathrm{ideal}}\rangle$ should obey the one-curve law that signal
does not. It does (Table~\ref{tab:collapse}): one shared exponential in
damage units fits the fidelity of both algorithms at $R^2=0.97$ (ladder)
and $0.99$ (depolarizing), with fitted amplitude ${\approx}1$ as the
zero-noise limit demands; granting the algorithms separate rates adds
only $0.02$/$0.003$. The decoder gap is visible in the raw numbers:
Grover's fidelity equals its signal to better than $0.006$ under the
ladder and $0.025$ under depolarizing at every point, while Shor's
$d=3$ family loses two thirds of its fidelity ($0.55\to0.18$) with
signal flat, and at $d=2$, $m=12$ under depolarizing, continued
fractions decode a signal of $0.131\pm0.018$ from a state with
fidelity $2.7(1.1)\times10^{-4}$. The mechanism claim, in the form the data
support: \emph{accumulated channel damage is the law for the quantum
state; the residual algorithm dependence of decoded success sits in
the decoder}---for which Sec.~\ref{sec:decoder} gives an exact
quantitative account, derived and verified against the decoder
itself.

\emph{What the collapse does and does not prove.} For a product of
near-identity incoherent channels, log-fidelity is additive in the
per-application entanglement infidelity to first order, so a single
exponential with amplitude ${\approx}1$ is the \emph{null
expectation}, not a discovery. The informative content of
Table~\ref{tab:collapse} is therefore bounded as well as measured. In
linear fidelity the shared fit gives $R^2=0.97/0.99$; rescored in
\emph{log} fidelity---the metric that weights the deep tail---the
same fit gives $R^2=0.76$ in both channels ($0.85$--$0.91$ if refit
there). The law holds within a factor of two down to fidelity
$1.5(6)\times10^{-2}$ on the ladder and, excepting a single
floor-pinned Grover point, to $2.7(1.1)\times10^{-4}$ under
depolarizing---though the endpoint fidelities themselves carry
$40$--$48\%$ statistical error at their $100$--$200$ trajectories,
so ``within a factor of two'' is at the resolution limit of the
deepest points;
every departure is a Grover point approaching its
$1/\!\dim\mathcal{H}$ fidelity floor (the deepest, at
$5.3\times10^{-3}$, sits $1.4\times$ above its floor of $1/256$ and
$82\times$ above the fit)---a floor that varies across exactly the
bases and sizes the collapse unifies. What survives everywhere is
the decoder residual, which no channel-level argument predicts. Conversely, the law's failure mode
is error that does not compose incoherently: correlated or coherent
noise breaks the additivity, and that is precisely what the deep
hardware circuit exhibits (Sec.~\ref{sec:hardware})---which is why
the law is stated for Markovian incoherent channels only (see
Limitations).

\subsection{Why the decoder gains tolerance with size}
\label{sec:decoder}

The size dependence of Shor's decoder tolerance has a closed form, and
because the decoder is classical we can measure it exactly rather than
argue it. Continued-fraction recovery succeeds for any outcome $y$
whose phase $y/D$ lies within $1/(2r^2)$ of some $s/r$---the classical
convergent guarantee~\cite{hardy2008,shor1997}. This sufficient
condition gives an acceptance window around each of the $r-1$ peaks
spanning $1/r^2$ in phase and holding ${\sim}D/r^2$ outcomes, while the
noiseless interference peak stays ${\sim}1$ outcome wide at every
size. The window therefore grows
linearly in $D=d^m$, exponentially in register size, whereas
noise-induced broadening grows only polynomially in $m$ (exposure $=$
carriers $\times$ layers). The decoder's redundancy therefore outpaces
the added exposure over a range of sizes, which is why Shor's signal
families hold a plateau while their fidelity decays exponentially---and
why Grover, whose acceptance set is the single marked item at every
size, shows no such plateau. The reprieve is finite: the acceptance set
grows linearly in $D$ but the accepted outcomes must still carry
amplitude, and once fidelity has fallen far enough the decoded signal
follows, which is the fall the sixth qutrit size exposes in
Sec.~\ref{sec:scaling}. Decoder tolerance postpones the decay of
decoded success; it does not repeal it.

Table~\ref{tab:decoder} runs the project's own continued-fraction
routine over every outcome $y\in[0,D)$ and counts the acceptance set
exactly; the growth from $1.6$ accepted outcomes at $D=64$ to $116$ at
$D=4096$ is a $73\times$ gain in tolerance against a peak that never
widens. The acceptance set moreover has an exact description. The
decoder accepts $y$ if and only if some convergent denominator $q$ of
$y/D$ is a multiple of $r$ with $q\le N$ (Appendix~\ref{app:decoder}
proves the equivalence and reconciles it with the divisor-form
recovery of the standard analyses), which reduces $A$ to pure
number theory: for a reduced fraction $p/q$, the phases with $p/q$
among their convergents form the open interval between the
Stern--Brocot mediants $(p+p')/(q+q')$ and $(2p-p')/(2q-q')$, where
$p'/q'$ is the penultimate convergent of $p/q$---a classical fact of
continued-fraction theory~\cite{khinchin1964,hardy2008}. A single phase can carry \emph{several} admissible convergent
denominators (at $N{=}55$, $r{=}5$ the chain $5,10,25$ is realizable,
$25=2\cdot10+5$), so the intervals for different admissible $q$
overlap and cannot simply be summed. Partitioning $A$ by the
\emph{first} admissible convergent of $y/D$ is disjoint by
construction, and restricting each interval to the phases whose
earlier convergents contain no admissible denominator gives the exact
finite-$D$ law: writing $\chi(p/q)=1$ when no convergent of $p/q$
before $q$ has denominator divisible by $r$,
\begin{equation}
\lvert A\rvert \;=\;
\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor N/r\rfloor}\;
\sum_{\substack{0<p<kr\\ \gcd(p,kr)=1\\ \chi(p/kr)=1}}
\Bigl\lvert\, D\cdot I\!\left(\tfrac{p}{kr}\right)\cap\mathbb{Z}
\,\Bigr\rvert,
\label{eq:decoderexact}
\end{equation}
with $I(p/q)$ the open mediant interval above. Equation
\eqref{eq:decoderexact} reproduces the enumerated $\lvert A\rvert$
\emph{without error}---outcome for outcome, on all 27 instance/size
combinations tested, including all four within-modulus orders. As
$D\to\infty$ each count becomes $D$ times an interval measure; the
exact per-denominator measure is
$\mu(q)=\tfrac{2}{q}\sum_{u<q,\,\gcd(u,q)=1}(q+u)^{-1}$, and summing
it over the admissible denominators,
$\lvert A\rvert/D\to\sum_{k}\mu(kr)$ minus the first-admissible
overlap corrections. The overlaps vanish on five of our six
instances---$r$ and $2r$ are never convergent denominators of the
same phase (if $q_j=r$ then $q_{j+1}=ar+q_{j-1}$ with
$0<q_{j-1}<r$ is not a multiple of $r$, and $q_{j+2}>2r$), and
deeper coincidences require the chain structure above---so there the
bare $\mu$-sum matches the enumerated limit to better than
$5\times10^{-5}$ relative; on the sixth ($N=55$, $r=5$, eleven
admissible denominators) the overlaps contribute $+3.5\%$. Replacing
$\mu(q)$ by its large-$q$ asymptote $2\ln2\,\varphi(q)/q^2$ gives the
totient form
\begin{equation}
\frac{\lvert A\rvert}{D}\;\approx\;
2\ln 2\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor N/r\rfloor}\frac{\varphi(kr)}{(kr)^2},
\label{eq:decoderlaw}
\end{equation}
with $\varphi$ Euler's totient (summed over \emph{all} denominators
$q\le Q$ the weight $\mu$ reproduces the almost-everywhere count
$(12\ln2/\pi^2)\ln Q$ of convergent denominators below
$Q$~\cite{khinchin1964}, so the measure itself is classical---the
content of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:decoderexact}--\eqref{eq:decoderlaw} is its
restriction to the denominators the decoder admits). The residual of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:decoderlaw} is dominated by the asymptote substitution,
not by finite-size discreteness: at the small denominators that carry
the sum, $2\ln2\,\varphi(q)/q^2$ errs by $\pm1$--$2\%$ in both
directions, and the measured deviations---between $-0.8\%$ and
$+0.7\%$ on five instances and $+4.1\%$ on the sixth, constant in $D$
over three decades---decompose accordingly (asymptote error on the
five overlap-free instances; asymptote plus overlap on the sixth). The law settles both scaling directions at once.
In $D$ it is exactly linear---the measured $\lvert A\rvert\propto
D^{1.03\pm0.01}$ ($R^2=0.999$) carries its small excess over slope 1
purely from window discreteness. In $r$ it replaces the $1/r^2$
envelope: comparing $r=5$ with $r=10$ on identical registers (the
within-modulus pairs of Sec.~\ref{sec:alignment}, swept to $D\ge5r^2$
so the window exceeds one outcome) gives measured per-peak ratios of
$9.6$ ($N=33$) and $8.9$ ($N=55$) where $1/r^2$ predicts $4.0$; the
law's totient-sum ratios are $9.7$ and $9.3$. It also explains the
envelope's apparent success in Table~\ref{tab:decoder} as an accident
of the benchmark instance: at $r=6$, $N=21$ the naive $(r-1)/r^2=0.139$
sits within $2\%$ of the law's $0.141$ because two errors
compensate---the true $q=r$ window measure $2\ln2\,\varphi(r)/r^2$ is
only $0.55\times$ the envelope's, and the admissible multiples
$2r,\dots,\lfloor N/r\rfloor r$ restore the difference. On the $r=5$
instances, where the cancellation fails, the envelope is off by
$2.5$--$3\times$ while the law stays within $4.2\%$.

\emph{Relation to success-probability analyses of order finding.} An
exact literature bounds the success of continued-fraction
post-processing on base-2 registers: Shor's original $4/\pi^2$
asymptotics~\cite{shor1997}, the sharp divisor-recovery bounds of
Gerjuoy~\cite{gerjuoy2005} and of Bourdon and
Williams~\cite{bourdon2007}, Eker{\aa}'s proof that a single run
recovers $r$ with probability approaching one~\cite{ekera2024}, the
tight two-sided window bounds of Magdon-Ismail and
Dong~\cite{magdon2025}, the self-contained treatise of Barzen and
Leymann~\cite{barzen2022}, and, for eigenstate phase estimation,
Chappell \emph{et al.}~\cite{chappell2011}. These analyses score
outcomes inside a tolerance window of the peaks via the convergent
guarantee---a sufficient condition---and typically certify recovery
of a divisor $r/\gcd(s,r)$ that a classical search then lifts to $r$.
The law above differs in kind: it is an exact, outcome-for-outcome
description of the full acceptance set of a decoder that must return
$r$ itself, including the contribution of the admissible denominators
$2r,\dots,\lfloor N/r\rfloor r$ that window bounds do not count.
Eq.~\eqref{eq:decoderlaw} depends only on $r$ and $N$, not on the
base: at matched control dimension the decoder's tolerance is
\emph{identical} across bases, so the entire cross-base difference in
decoded success sits in the quantum state---precisely this section's
mechanism claim. We are not aware of this acceptance set having been
computed before for a decoder required to return $r$ itself.

This decomposition has a general reading beyond the present comparison.
A decoded success probability conflates two quantities with different
physics: the decay of the quantum state, which damage-weighted exposure
describes universally, and the error tolerance of the classical
post-processing wrapped around the measurement, which is
algorithm-specific and can grow with problem size. Benchmarks of
``noise resilience'' across algorithms or encodings that use decoded
success as their metric are therefore partially measuring decoder
redundancy---a classical resource. Reporting end-state fidelity
alongside decoded signal, as in Table~\ref{tab:collapse}, separates the
two at no extra simulation cost, and we suggest it as standard practice
for noisy-algorithm comparisons.

\section{Robustness}
\label{sec:robustness}

\emph{Readout error.} A $d$-level readout must resolve $d$ pointer
states, the higher ones worst. Charging a readout channel with misread
rate of $\lvert k\rangle$ growing as $(1+k)$---a linear reading of the
few-percent, higher-is-worse readout errors reported for transmon
qutrits~\cite{blok2021,goss2022}---on every control carrier of every
base leaves the qudit advantage untouched: the ququint's lead drifts
by less than $\pm0.03$ over the full sweep $\varepsilon=0$--$0.04$ (an
$8\times$ range among nonzero settings). The reason is a structural cancellation: total readout
exposure is $m\times\varepsilon(d{+}1)/2$, and at matched precision
$m\approx\log D/\log d$, giving $9\varepsilon/8\varepsilon/9\varepsilon$
for $d=2/3/5$ at $D\approx64$. Per-level readout degradation with $d$ is
almost exactly cancelled by the reduced carrier count---near-neutral by
construction, not luck. The neutrality claim is scoped to
simultaneous $d$-level discrimination (the transmon case that
motivates the $(1{+}k)$ model): ion-qudit readout is sequential
shelving with $d-1$ detection rounds, so misreads compound down the
sequence and readout \emph{time} grows with $d$, adding idle
decoherence the cancellation above does not include.

\emph{Refocusing.} No transmon runs a long circuit without echo, so the
honest question is whom it helps more. Sweeping the dephasing scale from
free evolution to perfect echo (the same knob that models the
high-$E_J/E_C$ regime~\cite{wang2025}), the ququint's lead grows
monotonically in all four conditions tested. The consequential case is
linear-in-$d$ gate cost: the ququint \emph{loses} Shor without echo
($-0.026$) and \emph{wins} with it ($+0.191$); QPE moves from exact
parity to $+0.196$. Mechanistically, dynamical decoupling suppresses
dephasing---the part of the ladder channel scaling worst with $d$ (the
max-level law)---and leaves the gentler $k^{0.7}$ relaxation, in
agreement with qudit DD experiments~\cite{tripathi2025} and with the
refocused molecular-qudit QFT of Ref.~\cite{rubinosanz2025}. This is why
the central condition is stated ``at the device's operating dephasing
level'': refocusing buys roughly one cost model of headroom, and
benchmarks of qudit algorithms should be run on refocused devices.

\emph{Noise-inflation threshold.} The channels charge every base the
same per-layer strength; if higher-$d$ gates are additionally noisier
per layer, the condition acquires a threshold. Inflating the qudit's
per-layer strength alone, $s_d=f\,s_2$, and locating the crossing
$f^\ast$ at which the qudit's floor-corrected signal falls to the
qubit's (demo instance, exact density matrices, both operating
points): under native-gate cost $f^\ast=2.0$--$2.5$ on the calibrated
ladder and $2.6$ ($d{=}3$) to $4.5$ ($d{=}5$) under depolarizing;
under the linear \texttt{ion} cost $f^\ast=1.2$ ($d{=}3$, ladder) and
$1.6$ ($d{=}3,5$, depolarizing), the ladder ququint cell being
already lost at $f=1$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}). Read as hardware
guidance: a platform keeps the qudit advantage while its measured
qudit-to-qubit per-gate noise ratio stays below $f^\ast$ \emph{after}
the layer-count multiplier is charged---roughly $1.6\times$ for
today's trapped-ion pairing and $2.0$--$2.5\times$ for a
native-entangler transmon (the calibrated-ladder/\texttt{uniform}
pairing; the $4.5\times$ cell belongs to
depolarizing/\texttt{uniform}, a crossed pairing).

\emph{The threshold at measured fidelities.} The only native
two-qudit entangling gate characterized across $d=2,3,5$ on one
apparatus---the single-application light-shift gate
of Hrmo \emph{et al.}~\cite{hrmo2023}, fidelity $99.6(1)/98.7(2)/
93.7(3)\%$ at $d=2/3/5$---decides the second condition. Converting
a measured infidelity $\epsilon$ to the implied per-carrier-layer
strength must go through the damage identity of the channel being
inflated: a strength-$s$ layer deposits entanglement infidelity
$\Delta(d)\,s$ per carrier, with $\Delta=1-1/d^2$ for the
depolarizing channel but $\Delta=0.75/1.46/2.83$ at $d=2/3/5$ for
the calibrated ladder, whose higher levels already relax and
dephase faster (the damage units of Sec.~\ref{sec:mechanism}). So
$s=\epsilon/[2L\,\Delta(d)]$ and $f=s_d/s_2$; this conversion is
also what keeps the ladder from being charged twice for the same
decoherence, since $f$ then measures the gate's noise in units of
what the ambient ladder itself deposits during the gate's $L$
layers. (An earlier version of this analysis applied the
depolarizing identity to both channels, overstating the ladder
factors by $1.6\times$ at $d{=}3$ and $3.0\times$ at $d{=}5$.)
Under \texttt{uniform} accounting (the gate charged one layer, as
its single-application construction warrants) $f=1.67\pm0.49$ at
$d=3$ and $4.17\pm1.06$ at $d=5$ on the ladder, $2.74\pm0.80$ and
$12.30\pm3.13$ under depolarizing; under the \texttt{ion}
accounting (the $(d{-}1)$ layer charge absorbing part of the
growth) $0.83\pm0.24$ and $1.04\pm0.27$ on the ladder,
$1.37\pm0.40$ and $3.08\pm0.78$ under depolarizing ($1\sigma$
propagated from the quoted gate errors). The factor is measured on an entangling gate,
so \emph{where} it is charged is a modeling choice, and we bracket
it. Global inflation---every carrier-layer at $f s_2$, the scope of
$f^\ast$ above---charges the qudit's idle and single-qudit layers
(two-thirds of the $d=5$ exposure) a penalty measured only on its
entangler, which the flat measured single-qudit error and
${\sim}100$-ms shielded idle coherence above argue against;
gate-only inflation---the two participants of each entangling gate
at $f s_2$ through that gate's layers, the rest at
$s_2$---concedes the qudit an idle noise that does not grow with
$d$ at all. Rerunning the demo grid by exact density
matrices at these factors under both scopes
(Table~\ref{tab:hrmo}; \texttt{results/transmon\_rebuild.json},
\texttt{results/hrmo\_gate\_only.json}): \emph{the ququint
advantage is lost} globally in every channel/cost pairing---the
measured $f$ exceeds $f^\ast$ by $1.7\times$ to $2.7\times$
wherever $f^\ast$ is defined, and the ladder/\texttt{ion} cell was
lost at $f=1$ already---and gate-only in three of four pairings,
the exception being the most lenient reading available (ladder,
\texttt{uniform} cost, entangler-only charge), where it survives
centrally by $+0.04$ and dies at $+1\sigma$. \emph{The qutrit now
passes under both scopes}: globally it survives three of four
pairings (ladder $0.58$/$0.63$ vs the qubit's $0.51$ under
\texttt{uniform}/\texttt{ion} cost; per-particle depolarizing
under \texttt{ion} cost $0.38$ vs $0.33$) and loses only
depolarizing/\texttt{uniform}, centrally but within $1\sigma$
($0.32$ vs $0.33$); gate-only it survives \emph{all four}
($0.66$/$0.60$ on the ladder, $0.51$/$0.44$ vs $0.33$ under
depolarizing). At measured fidelities the second condition
therefore fails at $d=5$ in seven of eight scope/cost pairings,
with the eighth a near-tie, and passes at $d=3$ for the trapped-ion
gate in seven of eight---the scope question, a measurable property
rather than a modeling choice, has moved from deciding the ion
qutrit to deciding the transmon one below.

\emph{The transmon side of the same test.} The measured cross-Kerr
two-qutrit gates of Ref.~\cite{goss2022}---CZ$^\dagger$ process
fidelity $97.3(1)\%$, CZ $95.2(3)\%$---face the matched
calibrated-ladder/\texttt{uniform} pairing. Goss \emph{et al.} report
no qubit-subspace entangler on the same footing, so the qubit anchor
is the operating point $s_2=0.003$ itself, equivalent to a
same-class two-qubit gate at $99.55\%$; the ladder-consistent
inflation factors are then $f=3.08\pm0.11$ (CZ$^\dagger$) and
$5.47\pm0.34$ (CZ). The CZ fails both scopes even at $-1\sigma$.
The CZ$^\dagger$ splits them
(\texttt{results/transmon\_rebuild.json}): globally it is lost even
at $-1\sigma$ ($f>f^\ast=2.05$; signal $0.34$ against the qubit's
$0.51$), while gate-only it \emph{passes}, robustly at $\pm1\sigma$
($f=3.08<f^\ast=3.63$; signal $0.55$ vs $0.51$). Inverted, the
transmon targets are a two-qutrit entangler at ${\gtrsim}96.8\%$
(gate-only scope) to ${\gtrsim}98.2\%$ (global) against $97.3\%$
measured: the measured gate sits \emph{inside} the bracket, and the
scope question---whether idle decoherence scales with
entangling-gate error---now decides the transmon qutrit exactly as
it once decided the ion one. On this platform the physics leans to
the harsher reading: the charge dispersion of $\lvert2\rangle$ that
drives idle dephasing is the same mechanism that limits the
cross-Kerr entangler~\cite{blok2021,goss2022}, so idle and gate
error should co-scale---the global scope---and the operative target
is $98.2\%$, $0.9$ points above the measured gate. The verdict is
also a function of the assumed anchor: re-anchored to a
Willow-class $99.67\%$ two-qubit gate the factor rises to $f=4.20$
against $f^\ast_{\rm gate}=3.57$ and the CZ$^\dagger$ fails both
scopes; anchors at $99.34\%$ and $99.0\%$ give $f=2.10$ and $1.38$
against thresholds that move far less, passing gate-only
comfortably and, at $99.0\%$, the global scope as well. The
required two-qutrit fidelity is therefore a schedule against the
comparator, not one number: $97.7$--$98.7\%$ against Willow-class
gates down to $92.3$--$95.7\%$ against a $99.0\%$-class device
(gate-only to global scope;
\texttt{results/transmon\_rebuild.json} sweeps four anchors with
$f^\ast$ recomputed at each operating point).
Inverting the threshold gives the experimental target: under
\texttt{ion} accounting the ququint returns to advantage when its
native-gate infidelity falls below
${\approx}f^\ast\cdot 4\,(0.96/0.75)\,\epsilon_2\approx0.033$ at
$\epsilon_2=0.004$, i.e.\ a $d=5$ native entangler at ${\gtrsim}96.7\%$
fidelity---against $93.7\%$ measured.

\emph{Wall-clock exposure.} The layer-counted convention above
charges the $580$-ns cross-Kerr gate one layer---the same as a
$30$-ns single-qutrit pulse. The opposite convention charges every
gate its duration, in units of a reference $d=2$ two-qubit gate:
$(0.2,1.0)$ layers for the qubit register's single/two-qubit gates,
$(0.3,\rho)$ for the qutrit's with $\rho=t_{2q}(3)/t_{2q}(2)$,
every carrier idling through every gate at equal ambient strength
per unit time and \emph{no} separate inflation factor---in this
convention the entangler's decoherence is charged as elapsed time,
and charging $f$ on top would double-count from the other side
(at the operating point, $2\rho\,\Delta(3)\,s_2=0.051$ of ambient
damage at $\rho=5.8$ already exceeds the whole measured infidelity
$0.027$). Sweeping $\rho$ on the demo instance by exact density
matrices (\texttt{results/transmon\_rebuild.json}), the qutrit's
timed margin over the qubit falls from $+0.21$ at $\rho=1$ through
$+0.04$ at $\rho=2$ to $-0.35$ at $\rho=5.8$, crossing zero at
$\rho^\ast=2.3$: charged its true duration, the Goss gate keeps the
advantage only against a two-qubit comparator slower than
${\sim}250$~ns---an IBM-class cross-resonance gate
($\rho\approx1.9$), not a $100$-ns CZ ($\rho=5.8$), and not a
Google-class $30$-ns gate ($\rho\approx19$, margin $-0.71$). The
layer-counted and timed conventions bracket the physical schedule
from opposite sides; that the transmon qutrit passes one and fails
the other at today's gate times is the honest state of the
platform, and the $d\ge5$ verdicts only worsen here, no transmon
entangler beyond $d=3$ having been demonstrated at
all~\cite{goss2022}.

\emph{Leakage.} The ladder channel is trace-preserving on the
computational manifold; a real cross-Kerr gate is not, and part of
the measured $\epsilon_3$ is leakage out of
$\{\lvert0\rangle,\lvert1\rangle,\lvert2\rangle\}$. If a fraction
$\lambda$ leaks, the in-manifold inflation drops to $(1-\lambda)f$
while the leaked population, scored at the decoder floor,
multiplies the signal by ${\sim}(1-\lambda\epsilon_3)^{18}$ over
the demo circuit's $18$ two-qutrit interactions. At the
$\lambda=\tfrac12$ endpoint this trades $f=3.08\to1.54$ against a
$0.78$ survival factor: the gate-only pass thins from $0.55$ to
$0.53$ against the qubit's $0.51$ and the global loss stands
($0.47$). Leakage does not flip either verdict at the demo depth,
but it is a qudit-only charge with no qubit counterpart, it
compounds with depth ahead of any in-manifold error, and its
measured fraction on this gate is the single most useful number a
transmon follow-up could report.

\emph{Ladder-exponent sensitivity.} The calibrated ladder fixes
relaxation at $\Gamma_k\propto k^{0.7}$ and dephasing at
$\max(j,k)^{1.1}$. Both exponents are constrained mainly by $d=3$ pair
data (Blok's $1:2.0:2.3$, which the channel realizes as
$1:2.14:2.14$), and are then applied at $d=5$ and $7$. A provenance
caveat sharpens the stakes: if those pair ratios were extracted from
Ramsey decay in a quasi-static-dominated regime---where decay is
Gaussian and $1/T_2^\ast$ scales with the \emph{first} power of the
level sensitivity---then the Lindblad rates realize their
\emph{squares}, $1:4.0:5.3$, a steepening the sweep below prices at
dephasing exponent ${\approx}2$. Peterer
\emph{et al.}~\cite{peterer2015}, who measured the sequential ladder to
the fourth level, disagree in both places: their $T_1$ series gives
$\Gamma_{21}/\Gamma_{10}=2.05$ (i.e.\ $k^{1.0}$, not $k^{0.7}$), and
their $T_2$ series gives pair rates $1:2.25:6.0:{>}36$ against
$1:2.14:3.35:4.59$ under the max-level law. No power law fits that
escalation---exponent $1.6$ matches the $2{\leftrightarrow}3$ pair,
$2.6$ matches $3{\leftrightarrow}4$, neither matches both---so rather
than refit we sweep the exponent across the range Peterer admits and
ask what survives (Table~\ref{tab:exponent}). The $d=2$ column is a
control: the channel is normalized on the $0{\leftrightarrow}1$
subspace, so every $d=2$ cell must be invariant, and is.

\emph{Concurrent schedules.} Every result here is an exposure budget,
and the budget charges every carrier an idle channel through every
gate. That serial convention is not neutral: concurrent execution
shortens the schedule most for the register with the most carriers,
which is $d=2$, so the assumption runs \emph{against} the conclusion it
supports and deserves a measurement rather than a disclaimer. Packing
the gate list by as-soon-as-possible list scheduling on carrier
availability---exact, since gates on disjoint carriers
commute---compresses the demo circuits by
$26.3\%/23.1\%/20.0\%$ at $d=2/3/5$ under \texttt{uniform} cost,
$26.3\%/15.9\%/7.1\%$ under \texttt{ion}, and
$26.3\%/14.9\%/4.8\%$ under \texttt{pavlidis}, confirming the
predicted
direction: the qubit gains most, and under the surcharged costs the
ququint gains almost nothing. Re-running the six cells of
Table~\ref{tab:cost} under the
parallel schedule leaves five of the six qudit-vs-qubit verdicts
unchanged while every margin erodes: $+0.452\to+0.385$ and
$+0.172\to+0.113$ (depolarizing, \texttt{uniform}/\texttt{ion}),
$+0.349\to+0.268$ and $+0.092\to+0.023$ (ladder), and
$+0.130\to+0.076$ (depolarizing/\texttt{pavlidis}). The one flip is
the ladder/\texttt{pavlidis} cell, whose thin serial qutrit win
($+0.051$) becomes a thin qubit win ($-0.020$)---the same cell the
single-qudit charge below eliminates, by the same logic: its serial
margin lives on uncharged qudit layers. Two further
readings follow. The serial convention inflates the qudit margin by up
to a factor of four but does not create it where the win is real; and
the ladder/\texttt{ion}
cell, at $+0.023$ under concurrency, is thin enough that it should be
read as a tie rather than a win. The depolarizing/\texttt{ion} cell
independently reproduces the near-tie found in the strength sweep
above: under concurrency its best qudit switches from $d=5$ to $d=3$.

\emph{Single-qudit gate cost.} Both \texttt{uniform} and \texttt{ion}
charge a single-qudit gate one layer at every $d$. Per laser pulse that
matches Ref.~\cite{ringbauer2022}; per \emph{operation} it does not,
since the same device's randomized benchmarking gives a per-Clifford
error rising $5\times$ from $d=3$ to $d=5$ on ${\sim}3\times$ the
pulses. The omission matters more than it looks, because the
single-qudit share of the schedule \emph{grows} with $d$: the
order-finding circuit spends $2m$ of its layers on single-qudit
Fourier rotations, which is $21\%/31\%/40\%$ of the serial schedule at
$d=2/3/5$. A $d$-dependent charge therefore hits the qudit twice.
Charging single-qudit gates $\mu_1(d)=(d/2)^\alpha$ layers, normalized
to $1$ at $d=2$ like every other multiplier here, and sweeping
$\alpha$, the measurement pins the $d{=}3:d{=}5$ ratio to
$\alpha=2.15$ (pulse count) or $\alpha=3.15$ (per-Clifford error);
the $d=2$ end is an extrapolation, which is why this is reported as a
threshold rather than a table row. Four of the six cells survive the
steeper calibration: depolarizing under \texttt{uniform}
($\alpha^\ast$ beyond the swept range), \texttt{ion}
($\alpha^\ast=3.55$), and---by a hair---\texttt{pavlidis}
($\alpha^\ast=3.17$, margin $+0.130\to+0.056$ at the pulse-count
charge and $+0.001$ at the per-Clifford charge), and the ladder under
\texttt{uniform}
($\alpha^\ast=3.84$). Two do not, and both are ladder cells. The
ladder/\texttt{ion} cell has $\alpha^\ast=2.14$---it fails at
\emph{exactly} the measured pulse-count charge, margin
$+0.092\to-0.001$. And the ladder/\texttt{pavlidis} cell, whose
serial qutrit win is new to the corrected decomposition charge, has
$\alpha^\ast=1.44$ and does not survive any
measured charge ($+0.051\to-0.036$ at $\alpha=2.15$)---the same cell
concurrency flips, for the same reason. The
depolarizing/\texttt{pavlidis} survival is the analysis's most
delicate verdict and we read it as a tie at the steep end rather
than a win. Across the whole
swept range the best qudit is $d=3$ in every cell from
$\alpha\ge1.5$---by $\alpha=3.15$ the ququint has fallen below the
qubit even under native-gate cost---which is the same conclusion the
ladder-exponent and measured-fidelity analyses reach independently.

\emph{Zeeman-structured dephasing.} The per-particle convention
flattens pair structure the ion encoding really has, so we also test
its worst case: magnetic-field dephasing carrying the collective-$B$
sensitivity structure of the $^{40}$Ca$^{+}$ level indexing of
Ref.~\cite{ringbauer2022}, applied independently per carrier---the
uncorrelated worst case---realized exactly by a single diagonal jump
$\propto\mathrm{diag}(g_j m_j)$ ($g_S=2$, $g_D=6/5$; the Markovian
generator stands in for the unrefocused effect of what is, in the
laboratory, quasi-static noise), normalized so the optical-qubit pair
$0{\leftrightarrow}1$ matches the qubit channel. Pair rates then span
$1$--$25\times$ that reference at $d=3$ and $1$--$49\times$ at $d=5$.
The encoding is no strawman: over all $\binom{8}{d}$ level subsets of
the $S_{1/2}$/$D_{5/2}$ manifold, the chosen levels exactly minimize
the worst pair rate at $d=5$ and $d=7$; only $d=3$ admits a gentler
choice ($9\times$, via $\{S_{-1/2},D_{-3/2},D_{-1/2}\}$).

The verdict reverses outright (Table~\ref{tab:zeeman}): the qubit
wins at every strength under both cost models. Nor is the missing
correlation structure doing the work: repeating the study with
genuinely common-mode dephasing---one global jump coupling to the
total Zeeman shift, exact as an elementwise mask on the density
matrix---preserves the reversal; its decoherence-free pairs soften
every base, the qubit most. Unmitigated \emph{Markovian}
Zeeman-structured dephasing on a sensitivity-ordered encoding is
therefore a regime with no qudit advantage at all---the sharpest
failure mode we found, and one the condition's ``operating dephasing
level'' clause must be read to include. That qualifier carries the
weight, however: the Lindblad generator is a stand-in for noise that
is quasi-static, and replacing it with the real thing changes the
answer in the qudit's favor.

\emph{Refocusing is $d$-dependent on this encoding.} A static field
offset is removed by permuting levels: a sequence
$\pi_1,\dots,\pi_L$, each held for an equal share of the idle window
and undone at the end, leaves level $j$ with mean coefficient
$\tilde c_j=L^{-1}\sum_l c_{\pi_l(j)}$, and refocuses exactly iff
$\tilde c$ is constant in $j$. For the $^{40}$Ca$^{+}$ coefficients
this has a sharp answer. At $d=2$ the level swap gives $\tilde c$
constant: one $\pi$-pulse cancels the qubit's dephasing exactly, as
the standard argument says. At $d\ge3$ \emph{no} two-interval echo
refocuses, for any permutation; the best leaves a residual pair
splitting of $1.5\times$ ($d=3$) or $1.0\times$ ($d=5$, $d=7$) the
\emph{unrefocused qubit} splitting. Exact refocusing needs $L=d$
intervals---the cyclic shift---and $d$ is minimal over all
permutation sequences, not merely over powers of one pulse
(exhaustive search, $d=3$ and $d=5$). The mitigation that dismisses
this regime is therefore free for the qubit and costs $d$ pulses for
the qudit, which is the opposite of the $d$-independence the
constant-overhead passive-decoupling
construction~\cite{sutherland2023} would supply, and the reason that
construction is the right thing to reach for here.

\emph{The Markovian stand-in was not conservative.} Simulating the
field as it is---one offset per shot, drawn from a Gaussian and held
fixed, integrated over that Gaussian by quadrature rather than
sampled---moves every unmitigated cell toward the qudit
(Table~\ref{tab:quasistatic}). Under native-gate cost the verdict goes
from near-parity ($0.275$ vs $0.299$) to a clear ququint win ($0.296$
vs $0.605$); under linear cost it goes from an outright qubit win
($0.275$ vs $0.160$) to a tie ($0.296$ vs $0.304$). The mechanism is
the exponent, not the scale: quasi-static damage accumulates as the
\emph{square} of circuit depth, and once $\sigma$ is pinned on the
qubit's $57$-layer schedule the shallower qudit schedules ($26$ and
$15$ layers under \texttt{uniform}) are rewarded superlinearly. The
reversal reported above is thus an artifact of the substitution the
manuscript flagged, and we withdraw it as a claim about laboratory
field noise while retaining it as what it is---a Markovian worst case.
Two internal checks support the arithmetic: the $d$-pulse rows
reproduce the pure-depolarizing signals of Table~\ref{tab:cost}
exactly, as they must once the Zeeman component is cancelled, and the
quadrature is converged to $2\times10^{-14}$ under halving of its node
spacing.

\emph{Charging the pulses returns the advantage to $d=3$.} Idealizing
refocusing pulses as free---the most generous reading of the passive
constructions---the ququint leads in every mitigated cell. Charging
each pulse one layer of exposure, which is what the naive
$d$-interval sequence costs, reverses that: $d=3$ overtakes $d=5$
under both cost models ($0.387$ vs $0.361$ and $0.283$ vs $0.232$),
while both stay above the qubit's $0.173$. This is the fourth
independent route in this paper to the same place---the qutrit, not
the ququint, is what survives realistic accounting.

Measured devices are engineered away from this regime: shielding
makes coherence times of order $100$\,ms achievable across all
transitions~\cite{ringbauer2022}. The device's randomized
benchmarking---per-pulse error rising only $1.6\times$ from $d=3$ to
$d=5$ ($2.0\times10^{-4}$ to $3.2\times10^{-4}$) where the raw
sensitivity ratio is $25$--$49$---is consistent with a near-flat
residual, though a few-microsecond pulse cannot resolve slow
dephasing; the direct evidence the convention needs is $d$-resolved
Ramsey or echo coherence across the encoding's transitions, and
pending that, the per-particle convention is a statement about
mitigated hardware whose price of failure the following sweep
fixes. The mechanism mirrors the refocusing result
above---structured, slow
dephasing is exactly the part echoes remove---and the price is
quantitative: composing the Zeeman component with the depolarizing
operating point ($s=0.005$; the two channels commute, so the product
is exact) and sweeping the suppression factor $\varepsilon$ applied
to the unmitigated Zeeman reference ($s_Z=0.003$ per carrier-layer),
the qudit ordering returns at $\varepsilon^\ast=0.58$--$0.79$ under
native-gate cost but only at $\varepsilon^\ast=0.09$--$0.15$ under
the $2(d{-}1)$ M{\o}lmer--S{\o}rensen cost. In hardware units the bar
is a $0{\leftrightarrow}1$-pair coherence of ${\gtrsim}400$--$600$
layer times for native-gate cost and ${\gtrsim}2200$--$3800$ for MS
cost: at ${\sim}100\,\mu$s layers, a $100$\,ms shielded coherence
meets the native-gate bar with a factor-two margin but falls short
of the MS-cost bar by $2$--$4\times$. On linear-cost hardware the
Zeeman component must be suppressed below the plain shielding
level---deeper echo sequences, passive decoupling of the full
manifold~\cite{sutherland2023}, or magnetically insensitive
encodings---before the qudit ordering returns; this
$\varepsilon^\ast=0.09$--$0.15$ bar is the actionable specification
this subsection contributes to the experimental proposal of
Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\emph{Composite dimension.} Nothing in the circuits or channels uses
primality. A $d=4$ point on the demo instance lands in the qudit band
($0.83$/$0.75$ against $0.73$/$0.67$ at $d=3$ and $0.76$/$0.78$ at
$d=5$), but $d=4$ is a prime power---GF(4) exists, so the
fault-tolerant machinery does not break there---and its $D=64$
register shares the qubit's residual misalignment bit for bit, so it
cannot settle the question alone. The decisive control is
$d=6$---composite, non-prime-power, no field structure---run on the
alignment-neutral instance ($N=29$, $r=7$; residual misalignment
$0.2857$, identical for every base $d=2$--$6$; 1000 trajectories per
point): the family reads $0.44/0.62/0.73/0.71/0.77$
($d=2/3/4/5/6$, calibrated ladder) and $0.28/0.53/0.72/0.73/0.83$
(depolarizing), both composites inside the qudit band and $d=6$ at
its top under depolarizing. The bare-circuit dynamics carries no
trace of primality; the prime restriction elsewhere in this paper is
inherited from the fault-tolerance and QFT-arithmetic
motivations~\cite{gottesman1999,campbell2012,floratos2024}.

\emph{Independent validation.} Beyond internal checks
(Sec.~\ref{sec:methods}), we reproduce the three central equations of
Ref.~\cite{jankovic2024}---qudit and multi-qubit process infidelities
and the critical curve $(d^2-1)/(3\log_2 d)$---from our superoperator
code with no analytics of our own, to a worst relative error of
$4.1\times10^{-4}$ over $d=2$--$64$, with the residual identified as
their first-order truncation (it tracks the infidelity itself).

\section{Hardware anchor}
\label{sec:hardware}

The simulation stack makes falsifiable predictions, and the qubit rows
of Table~\ref{tab:prediction} compile at face-value gate counts on
cloud hardware: with the work register reduced to one qubit prepared
in the eigenstate, each controlled-$U^{2^i}$ is a single
controlled-phase, and the no-swap inverse QFT adds $m(m-1)/2$
more---15 entangling gates at $m=5$ (6 qubits), 28 at $m=7$ (8
qubits). We ran these circuits on two commercial devices
(Table~\ref{tab:hardware}): the trapped-ion IonQ Forte-1
(all-to-all connectivity, vendor-reported two-qubit
$ZZ$ fidelity $0.993$) and the 20-qubit square-lattice superconducting
IQM Garnet. Predictions were recomputed for the compiled circuits by
exact density-matrix evolution under the paper's per-particle
depolarizing convention, bracketed by two exposure accountings (every
gate a full noise layer, or single-qubit gates charged their measured
$130/970$ duration ratio).

Three findings. \emph{First, the shallow ion circuit fixes the
channel's scale to within the conversion ambiguity}: the measured
$0.617\pm0.007$ sits inside the $0.60$--$0.70$ band when the vendor
infidelity is read directly as the per-layer strength ($s=0.007$),
and ${\approx}0.07$ \emph{below} the $0.69$--$0.76$ band that follows
from the paper's own gate-to-strength conversion
$s=\epsilon/1.5=0.0047$ (the convention Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}
applies to the Hrmo gate)---the two readings differ by exactly the
factor $2L(1-1/d^2)$, and one scalar cannot decide between them.
Debiasing is
an error-mitigation ensemble average over qubit mappings and is not
doing the work here: the raw 1{,}000-shot pilot scored
$0.608\pm0.015$, within $0.009$ of the debiased value.
Because the
success-vs-strength map is steep, this single number is consistent
with an effective per-gate depolarizing strength of
$0.005$--$0.010$ across the two exposure conventions, a range that
brackets the vendor's measured $0.7\%$ two-qubit infidelity but sits
above the $0.0047$ our own conversion assigns it. We read
this as a single-point consistency check of the channel's
\emph{scale} at shallow depth---one scalar cannot validate the
channel's structure or pin the conversion,
and SPAM, single-qubit error, and idle decoherence are all absorbed
into the one fitted parameter, plausibly accounting for the excess
over the converted prediction---while the two deeper circuits below
bound the depth range over which the check holds. \emph{Second, the deep ion circuit fails coherently, not by
decoherence.} At $m=7$ the interference peak is destroyed
($0.011$, below the random floor of $0.031$) while the work qubit
remains at $0.99$; no relabeling of the outcome bits recovers the
peak (the best over 10{,}080 reinterpretations---every ordering of
the control bits in both polarities---reaches only $0.15$, not
significant over so large a hypothesis set), and a 500-shot raw $m=4$
probe caught the mechanism directly, unmitigated---a
nearly pure output state ($71\%$ of shots on one outcome) whose phase
is wrong by one least-significant digit. Debiasing does not remove
the failure the raw probe establishes, and an approximate-QFT variant that
drops the three smallest rotation angles recovers only a factor
${\sim}6$ ($0.066$ vs a $0.44$--$0.57$ band): the deep no-swap QFT
fails wholesale on this device, consistent with accumulated coherent
angle systematics rather than the incoherent noise our channels
describe. (A base-2 datum adjacent to the truncation-robustness
conjecture for qudits~\cite{pavlidis2021}---which it cannot test
directly: truncation buys an order of magnitude but cannot repair a
coherent failure.) \emph{Third, the superconducting lattice fails by plain
decoherence}: both circuits sit at the random floor with the work
qubit visibly decayed ($0.81$/$0.66$), the signature of SWAP-routing
the all-to-all kickback pattern through a fixed-connectivity chip
whose coherence time is four to five orders shorter than the ions'.

The three-way outcome maps onto the paper's framework. The
per-particle depolarizing convention quantitatively describes the
platform it was calibrated to---provided the circuit is shallow enough
that coherent systematics stay subdominant, the regime our Limitations
already fence off. The superconducting failure is compilation
overhead deciding the outcome before noise structure enters, the
hardware face of the \texttt{pavlidis} lesson. And the deep-circuit
ion failure is a caution for any decoded-success benchmark on NISQ
hardware: past a depth threshold, what is measured is coherent
calibration error, not the decoherence such benchmarks nominally
probe. One practical note for reproduction: IonQ returns the control
register digit-reversed relative to the Braket local-simulator
convention (the $m=5$ peak sits exactly on the bit-reversed ideal
outcome); all scores above use that pre-registered reading.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\emph{Which platform first.} Both physically matched cost/noise
pairings favor ququints under their mitigated operating conventions,
but the two conditions now pull the platforms apart.
Structurally, the native-entangler route is the robust one: it
tolerates a Zeeman component up to $\varepsilon\approx0.6$--$0.8$ of
the unmitigated reference where the linear-MS-cost route tolerates
only $0.09$--$0.15$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}). At measured
fidelities the ion qutrit is the stronger case: its gate passes
globally in three of four pairings and gate-only in all four,
while the transmon entangler passes only the gate-only scope
($f=3.08\pm0.11$ against $f^\ast_{\rm gate}=3.63$ but
$f^\ast_{\rm glob}=2.05$; Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness})---a scope the
platform's shared charge-dispersion mechanism argues against---and
only under layer-counted exposure, its advantage surviving
wall-clock charging only against two-qubit comparators slower than
${\sim}250$~ns. The structurally robust route thus waits on either
a ${\gtrsim}98.2\%$ two-qutrit entangler at its own anchor or a
demonstration that transmon idle noise does not track entangler
error. The
ion route also offers the larger headline gains on eigenstate QPE,
conditional on collective-$B$ suppression beyond plain shielding---a
requirement that belongs to the experimental specification below. The cheapest
decisive experiment we can propose is eigenstate QPE at $m=2$--$3$ on
a Ringbauer-class ion processor~\cite{ringbauer2022}, where
multi-qudit entangling algorithms have been demonstrated at $d=3$ and
$d=5$ (a qutrit--ququint lattice-gauge simulation~\cite{meth2025};
earlier $d=3$ circuit work in Refs.~\cite{roy2022,shi2025});
single-qudit demonstrations reach $d=8$ on a trapped
ion~\cite{shi2025} and, on photonic platforms, $d=32$ Shor and qudit
QPE~\cite{weng2024,lu2020}. Table~\ref{tab:prediction} supplies the
predicted success probabilities at common per-base strengths,
computed by exact density-matrix evolution with no sampling error.
Read honestly against measured gates, however, the table must be
entered \emph{per base}, and the measured-fidelity analysis of
Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness} then predicts the $d=5$ experiment
\emph{fails} on today's hardware: at the strengths the Hrmo gate set
implies ($s_5=0.0082$ under the \texttt{ion} accounting,
$s_2=0.0027$ from the same apparatus's
$d=2$ gate), the qubit register wins both pairs---$0.657$ vs $0.599$
at 4 bits and $0.533$ vs $0.427$ at 5 bits
(\texttt{results/qpe\_measured\_strengths.json})---and the ququint
recovers the 4-bit pair only once its native-gate fidelity clears
${\approx}96.7\%$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}), against $93.7\%$
measured. The proposal therefore splits into a near-term and a
target-setting branch. Near term, the decisive test is the
\emph{qutrit}: at measured fidelities it survives \emph{both}
inflation scopes in three of four channel/cost pairings, including
the matched trapped-ion one (\texttt{ion} cost, per-particle
noise, $f=1.37<f^\ast=1.56$ globally, comfortable gate-only); the
margins are small enough that either
outcome is informative, and the circuits are shallower than anything
in Table~\ref{tab:prediction}. Charging each base the strength its own
measured gate implies ($s_3=0.0037$, $s_2=0.0027$), eigenstate QPE at
$d=3$, $m=4$ (ten entangling gates, $D=81$) is predicted to succeed at
$0.612$ against $0.533$ for the matched $d=2$, $m=7$ register
(twenty-eight gates, $D=128$); the qutrit's raw-success margin
survives the alternative $d=2$, $m=6$ pairing ($0.612$ vs $0.505$),
though its floor-corrected signal does not ($0.600$ vs $0.603$), the
coarser $D=64$ grid depressing that register's noiseless ceiling to
$0.816$. Raw success is the directly measured quantity and the one we
would score. The shallower $m=3$ pair is a dead heat by construction
($0.655$ vs $0.657$ raw, signal $0.734$ vs $0.695$) and serves as a
systematics check rather than a test
(\texttt{results/qpe\_d3\_measured.json}). For the ququint the table stands as a
falsifiable \emph{conditional} prediction: at matched per-base
strength $s=10^{-3}$ the $m=3$ pair separates $0.855$ vs $0.778$,
and the separation widens down the demonstrated-fidelity band
($0.575$ vs $0.328$ at $5\times10^{-3}$)---but reaching the regime
where both bases actually sit at a common $s$ requires the
$96.7\%$ gate target above, a concrete specification for the next
device generation rather than a run for the current one. The
deep-circuit coherent failure of Sec.~\ref{sec:hardware} counsels
entering at the shallow $m=2$ pair in either branch.

\emph{Relation to code-level comparisons.} Keppens \emph{et
al.}~\cite{keppens2025} find slightly \emph{worse} qudit logical error
rates for the five-qudit code under the same noise convention we use,
and the two results are compatible: a code is five carriers and a fixed gate list at
every $q$---no problem instance to compress---so raising $q$ buys no
width or depth while charging every carrier more. An algorithm
compresses; that compression is the entire source of the advantage
measured here. A qudit advantage must be argued at the level of a
compressible workload, and does not transfer automatically to the
error-correction layer. Relatedly, Bocharov \emph{et
al.}~\cite{bocharov2017} show emulated-binary arithmetic can beat native
ternary---encoding density alone is not a mechanism---which is
consistent with our attribution of the advantage to width-and-depth
compression under a per-carrier noise budget, and with its disappearance
when compilation buys the compression back.

\emph{Recommendations to the field.} Cross-dimension algorithm
comparisons should (i) randomize or report grid alignment (an
irrational target phase does it for QPE; for order finding, choose $r$
dividing no $d^m$ and report residual misalignment); (ii) match problem
size across bases by interpolation rather than comparing raw $d^n$
points; (iii) charge multi-qudit gates their decomposition depth; and
(iv) report the floor-to-baseline span of the metric (we require
${>}0.15$; Sec.~\ref{sec:setup} discloses our one grandfathered
exception), since small orders collapse the continued-fraction
metric's dynamic range. Each rule was learned by stepping on the
corresponding rake.

\emph{Limitations.} Our channels are Markovian and incoherent: coherent
errors (cross-Kerr shifts $\alpha\approx0.1$--$0.7$\,MHz, drive-induced
shifts) and leakage during gates are not modeled; correlated noise
enters only through the common-mode Zeeman test of
Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}.
Cross-Kerr in particular is known to be fast on unprotected
qutrits~\cite{goss2022}, and since the number of level pairs it can
act on grows with $d$, its omission plausibly favors qudits---the
sign of this bias runs against our conclusion, not for it. Gate cost
and gate error are treated as proportional (more layers $=$ more idle
decoherence); a model where longer gates achieve better per-operation
fidelity would soften the ion penalty, and the inflation threshold of
Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness} bounds the tolerance in the opposite
direction. The schedule is serial (every carrier idles during every
gate), matching single-addressed ion strings; platforms that execute
gates concurrently cut idle exposure most for the widest register,
i.e.\ for $d=2$. That bias is now measured rather than asserted
(Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}): under an exact as-soon-as-possible
schedule one cell of Table~\ref{tab:cost} changes verdict (the thin
ladder/\texttt{pavlidis} qutrit win), the rest hold with
margins shrunk by up to $4\times$. The scaling trends are measured
to Hilbert-space dimension $5.3\times10^{5}$ and stated as slopes with error
bars, not theorems: the
Shor sweep uses two instances ($N=21$, $r=6$ and $N=29$, $r=7$) at one
noise strength per regime, with 3--6 sizes per base. The ordering and
the qubit's fastest decay replicate across the instances; the
qutrit's \emph{rate} replicates only in the ladder channel
($1.7\sigma$ over matched sizes) and not under depolarizing
($3.7\sigma$), so individual per-base slopes carry instance-level
variance of order $0.03$/bit---larger than the earlier
size-mismatched comparison suggested. The qutrit families are
plateau-then-fall rather than linear, so their quoted slopes average a
shape: six sizes locate the fall in the last three but cannot fit its
onset, and the $N=29$ sweep stops before reaching it. Whether the
plateau lengthens, or the fall steepens, beyond 14.3 bits is
unmeasured. The qutrit's decoder account
is derived and verified exactly (Sec.~\ref{sec:decoder}) and grid
alignment is excluded as a confound. Statistical error
bars are small throughout; the dominant uncertainty is the systematic
spread across channel and cost-model choices, which is why we report
the full grid. Dimensions beyond $d=7$ are untested, and $d=7$ itself only at demo
size on one instance (Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}), where the break-even
window has visibly narrowed. All
simulations are of ideal algorithm circuits: the controlled-$U^{d^i}$
multipliers are applied as exact unitaries, each charged one gate at
the cost model's per-gate rate, whereas compiled modular
arithmetic---depth $4d^2q$ per adder under two-level
decomposition~\cite{pavlidis2021}---has a $d$-dependence of its own:
at face value the compiled qudit-to-qubit depth ratio spans
${\approx}0.9$--$2.7$ across $d=3,5$ (depending on whether adder- or
full-exponentiation depths dominate), extending well beyond the
\texttt{pavlidis} row's $0.85$--$1.09$, so compiled arithmetic can be
harsher than any cost model charged here and the decomposition
verdict is, if anything, conservative. Whether the penalty stays confined to
depth is only partially settled: the follow-up construction of
Ref.~\cite{floratos2024} gives an in-place, ancilla-free multiplier
of width exactly $n$ with nearest-neighbor interactions---but for
multiplication by a \emph{constant} modulo the register size $p^n$
(unit constants only), not the mod-$N$ modular multiplication Shor's
controlled-$U^{d^i}$ requires, which in general needs
comparison-and-correction machinery with ancillas of its own. Within
that scope compiled arithmetic inflates the layers term of the
carriers$\,\times\,$layers exposure budget (Sec.~\ref{sec:mechanism})
without touching the carrier count and maps onto a linear ion chain
without routing overhead; the width cost of full mod-$N$ arithmetic
on qudits remains open.
Fault-tolerant overhead
is out of scope, and per Ref.~\cite{keppens2025} its
dimension-dependence may differ in sign. Finally, $d=5$ entangling
gates exist today only on trapped ions~\cite{hrmo2023}---our
deep-register results are one to two hardware generations ahead of
experiment, and Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness} quantifies how far the
measured gate fidelities sit from the advantage regime.

\emph{Summary.} In bare, uncorrected circuits the qudit question has
a determinate answer, and it is doubly conditional. Width-and-depth
compression is the entire mechanism of the advantage: a native
entangling gate of at-most-linear cost preserves it; two-level
decomposition spends it back in proportion to dimension---fully at
$d\ge5$ on the ladder and at $d=7$ everywhere, only partly at
$d=3$, where the corrected charge leaves a thin advantage that
per-particle noise sustains and realistic ladder accounting
erases; the native gate's own infidelity growth
with $d$ can spend it back just as surely---at measured fidelities it
does, for the ququint, in seven of eight readings---and
Markovian-idealized structured
dephasing overwhelms it, though its laboratory quasi-static
counterpart does not. What the algorithm itself adds is classical---a
decoder whose error tolerance obeys an exact mediant-interval law
with totient-sum asymptotics, identical
across bases at matched control dimension---so the quantum state
carries the whole cross-base difference. Every cross-dimension
benchmark inherits these terms. The cheapest decisive test on current
hardware is eigenstate QPE at $d=3$ on a trapped-ion qudit
processor---a pairing measured fidelities leave alive under
both inflation scopes---while
the $d=5$ test waits on a native entangling gate at
${\gtrsim}96.7\%$ fidelity, a concrete target $3$ percentage points
above the demonstrated state of the art.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\emph{Simulators.} Exact density-matrix evolution (states as rank-$2n$
tensors, channels applied per carrier per layer) to Hilbert dimension
${\sim}3000$; beyond that, Monte Carlo wavefunction
trajectories~\cite{dalibard1992,molmer1993}: after
each gate, each carrier independently passes through the per-layer
channel raised to the gate's cost, sampled via one Kraus operator drawn
with probability $\mathrm{tr}(K^\dagger K\rho_q)$ from the carrier's
reduced state. Averaging $\lvert\psi\rangle\langle\psi\rvert$ over
trajectories reproduces the channel exactly; each trajectory contributes
its full outcome distribution, so statistical error is well below
Bernoulli---measured as a variance ratio $p(1{-}p)/\mathrm{var}(S_k)$
of $7.7$--$26.6$ over the five points characterized below.
That estimator is checked rather than asserted, on both counts a
$\sigma$-claim depends on. Unbiasedness: at $d=3$, $m=4$ under the
calibrated ladder, $8000$ trajectories give $0.24294\pm0.00092$
against an exact density matrix's $0.242474$, a $0.51\sigma$
agreement. Calibration: $24$ independent $1000$-trajectory replicas at
each of $(d,m)=(3,4)$, $(3,6)$, $(2,8)$, $(5,3)$ put the empirical
standard error of the mean against the quoted one at a ratio of
$0.995$ (range $0.84$--$1.17$; smallest Bartlett $p=0.22$), with
realized $1\sigma$ coverage $0.58$--$0.79$ around the nominal $0.68$.
The quoted bars are therefore neither conservative nor optimistic, and
every $\sigma$ and $\chi^2$ in Sec.~\ref{sec:scaling} is computed from
them by a committed script rather than by hand.

\emph{Reproducible seeds.} Every trajectory sweep now seeds from
\texttt{crc32} of its configuration tuple. It previously seeded from
\texttt{hash} of that tuple, and CPython salts string hashing per
process, so those seeds---and therefore the exact runs behind an
earlier release of these numbers---could not be regenerated. All
sweeps were re-run after the change. The re-run doubles as the
broadest calibration test in this work: old and new draw independent
streams at identical settings, so if the quoted standard errors are
honest the shifts $z=(\text{new}-\text{old})/\sqrt{\sigma_{\rm
new}^2+\sigma_{\rm old}^2}$ must be standard normal. Over the $368$
paired points they give mean $z=-0.09\pm0.05$ and
$\mathrm{sd}\,z=0.98\pm0.04$, with $5.2\%$ beyond $2\sigma$ against
$4.6\%$ expected---agreement over the whole grid, not just the five
points characterized above. Two conclusions moved, and both are stated
in their corrected form in the text: one of the six
grid-alignment predictions now misses by $1.4\sigma$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:alignment}), and the qutrit's depolarizing slope no longer
replicates across instances (Sec.~\ref{sec:scaling}). Every other
quoted trajectory number was re-synced to the post-reseed run for this
revision; none of those changes alters a verdict. No cost-table
or robustness verdict changed---those are exact density matrices and
carry no seed. Every paired shift is recorded in
\texttt{results/prehash\_comparison.json}; the size-scaling fits and
the qutrit/ququint crossings are regenerated by
\texttt{scaling\_claims.py}, and the entanglement-interpolation
slopes by \texttt{interpolation\_slopes.py}. Separately from the
re-seeding, an earlier version of the cost-model code charged
single-qudit gates the $d^2/4$ \texttt{pavlidis} multiplier that
both the text and Ref.~\cite{pavlidis2021}'s construction reserve
for controlled rotations; the corrected charge (single-qudit gates
one layer) re-scores every \texttt{pavlidis} cell---Table~\ref{tab:cost},
the $d=7$ grid, eigenstate QPE, Grover, and the concurrency and
single-qudit-cost analyses---and every such number in this version
reflects it. Fractional gate costs are exact: both channel families form
one-parameter semigroups, so $E^t$ is evaluated by scaling the Lindblad
rate (ladder) or as $1-q=(1-p)^t$ (depolarizing). End-state fidelity is
estimated as the trajectory average of
$\lvert\langle\psi_{\mathrm{ideal}}\vert\psi\rangle\rvert^2$, an
unbiased estimator of
$\langle\psi_{\mathrm{ideal}}\rvert\rho\lvert\psi_{\mathrm{ideal}}\rangle$,
cross-checked against exact density-matrix evolution. Throughout,
$\Fe=\mathrm{tr}\,S/d^2$ with $S$ the one-layer superoperator in the
natural representation; for a channel with Kraus operators $\{K_i\}$
this is $\sum_i\lvert\mathrm{tr}\,K_i\rvert^2/d^2$, the entanglement
fidelity of the channel with respect to the maximally entangled
state.

\emph{Calibrated dephasing via Euclidean embedding.} With diagonal jump
operators $\{J\}$, pairwise dephasing rates satisfy
$\Gamma_\phi(j,k)=\tfrac12\lVert v_j-v_k\rVert^2$ where $v_j$ collects
the $j$-th diagonal entries across jumps; realizing a target
$\Gamma_\phi$ matrix is therefore a Euclidean embedding problem, solved
exactly by classical multidimensional scaling (residual
$\le3\times10^{-17}$ for $d=2$--$7$). This realizes the measured
max-level law exactly---something no linear-frequency-ladder model can
do.

\emph{Verification.} Twenty correctness tests: QFT circuits against
dense matrices; complete positivity and trace preservation of every
channel (Choi eigenvalues); trajectory-vs-exact agreement;
fidelity-estimator cross-check; noiseless baselines; grid-alignment
predicates; unbiased-instance orderings; Grover correctness and cost
accounting; and readout-channel monotonicity. The exact decoder count of
Sec.~\ref{sec:decoder} and the formula of Eq.~\eqref{eq:decoderexact}
are verified by outcome-for-outcome comparison against decoder
enumeration on 27 instance/size combinations. The external
reproduction of Ref.~\cite{jankovic2024}
(Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}) runs separately from the test suite.
Statistics: 1000 trajectories per Shor and QPE scaling point;
400 per point for the $N=29$ instance-robustness
sweep (200 at the three deepest configurations, whose work registers
are wider than at $N=21$); 400 trajectories per point for the grid-alignment
and within-modulus studies; 1000 for the eigenstate-interpolation
study and for the $d=7$ demo grid; 200 for the fidelity-collapse Shor
points (100 at $d{=}2$,
$m{=}12$), whose Grover points use exact density-matrix evolution;
demo-size cost, SPAM, and refocusing results use exact density-matrix
evolution throughout, as does the multiplicative-group ensemble over
all eleven units of $\mathbb{Z}_{21}^{\times}$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:alignment}); its companions over all nineteen units
of $\mathbb{Z}_{33}^{\times}$ and all thirty-nine of
$\mathbb{Z}_{55}^{\times}$ use 400 trajectories per point. Error bars are standard errors over
trajectories; cross-base differences quoted with uncertainties are
propagated through the interpolation onto the common size axis.

\emph{Hardware runs.} Via AWS Braket (August 2026). IonQ Forte-1:
1{,}000-shot raw pilots, a 500-shot $m=4$ probe, and 5{,}000-shot main
runs with IonQ debiasing; IonQ's native $ZZ$ accepts
$\lvert\theta\rvert\le\pi/2$, so each controlled-phase compiles as
$R_z(\theta/2)\otimes R_z(\theta/2)\cdot ZZ(-\theta/2)$ with any
excess folded into paired $R_z(\pi)$ rotations---every compiled
variant reproduces the analytic outcome distribution on the Braket
local simulator to $10^{-15}$ before submission. IQM Garnet:
5{,}000 shots per circuit, server-side routing, native
controlled-phase support. Success windows, floors, and the
digit-reversed scoring are identical to the simulation pipeline;
per-task records, raw outcome histograms, and scoring code ship with
the repository.

\emph{Reproducibility.} All code, calibration fits, result files, and
the exact scripts that produce every figure and table are available at

\texttt{v1.0} snapshot is archived at \doi{10.5281/zenodo.21901533},
and the repository carries the current version, including the

\section{The decoder acceptance lemma}
\label{app:decoder}

The decoder used throughout this paper is the following procedure. On
outcome $y\in[1,D)$, scan the continued-fraction convergents $p/q$ of
$y/D$ in order of increasing denominator; at the first $q\le N$ with
$a^{q}\equiv1 \pmod N$, return the least $r'$ dividing $q$ with
$a^{r'}\equiv1 \pmod N$; if no convergent denominator $q\le N$ passes
the test, reject ($y=0$ is always rejected).

\emph{Lemma.} The decoder returns the order $r$ of $a$ modulo $N$ if
and only if some convergent denominator $q$ of $y/D$ satisfies $q\le
N$ and $r\mid q$; on every other outcome it rejects. In particular it
never returns a wrong order.

\emph{Proof.} Since $r$ is the multiplicative order of $a$ modulo
$N$, $a^{q}\equiv1\pmod N$ if and only if $r\mid q$. ($\Leftarrow$) Suppose some convergent denominator
$q\le N$ is a multiple of $r$; the scan stops at the first such $q$.
Every divisor $r'$ of $q$ with $a^{r'}\equiv1$ is itself a multiple of
$r$, and $r$ both divides $q$ and passes the test, so the least such
$r'$ is exactly $r$. ($\Rightarrow$) If no convergent denominator
$q\le N$ is a multiple of $r$, the test $a^{q}\equiv1$ never fires and
the decoder rejects. \hfill$\square$

The equivalence is verified empirically by
\texttt{decoder\_formula.py} in the repository: enumerating the
decoder over every outcome $y\in[0,D)$ and comparing against the
convergent predicate gives bit-identical acceptance sets on 42
(instance, $D$) combinations---six instances ($N=21$, $29$, and the
four within-modulus orders of $N=33,55$) across seven control
dimensions each.

\emph{Relation to the divisor form.} Standard analyses certify a
different event: for $y$ within $1/(2\tilde r^{2})$ of a peak $s/r$,
the convergent guarantee~\cite{hardy2008} yields the reduced fraction
$(s/g)/(r/g)$ with $g=\gcd(s,r)$, whose denominator $\tilde r=r/g$
\emph{divides} $r$~\cite{shor1997,ekera2024}. A divisor with $g>1$
fails the test $a^{\tilde r}\equiv1$, so those analyses lift $\tilde
r$ to $r$ by classical search over multiples (or over
gcd-smoothness classes~\cite{ekera2024}). The decoder above performs
no lift: it succeeds precisely through the convergent denominators
that are already multiples of $r$---including the deeper convergents
$kr\le N$, $k\ge2$, outside the sufficient window---which is why its
acceptance set obeys the totient sum of Eq.~\eqref{eq:decoderlaw}
over the admissible denominators $r,2r,\dots,\lfloor N/r\rfloor r$
rather than a window count.
